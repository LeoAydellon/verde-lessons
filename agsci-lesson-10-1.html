Unit 10 · Agriscience III

Lesson 10.1 — Running the Business Side

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

Every unit so far taught you how to GROW something — plants, animals, structures. This one teaches you how to not go broke doing it. The best hay grower in the county still loses the farm if the loan payments outrun the hay checks. Agriculture is a business, and business runs on three things: records that tell the truth, money managed on purpose, and marketing that finds a buyer before the product is sitting in the barn. You already did the production math — Lesson 7.3's feed batches, Lesson 9.1's bill of materials, Lesson 6.3's price-per-pound-of-nutrient. This lesson is where all that math starts earning.

Big idea: "profitable" is not a feeling — it's a number on an income statement. If the records don't show it, you don't know it. That's Lesson 2.3's evidence rule wearing a green eyeshade.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Capital — money or property put to WORK in a business.
  • Assets — what you own. Liabilities — what you owe.
  • Net worth (owner's equity) — assets minus liabilities. If assets outweigh debts, the business is solvent.
  • Budget — a written plan for income and expenses over a period.
  • Entrepreneurship SAE — you OWN the operation and carry the risk (your own show pigs). Placement SAE — you work for wages (the feed-store job).
  • Balance sheet — a snapshot on ONE date: assets, liabilities, net worth.
  • Income statement — covers a PERIOD: income minus expenses = profit or loss.
  • Cash flow statement — WHEN the money actually moves.
  • Depreciation — spreading a big purchase's cost over its working years. Land never depreciates — it doesn't wear out.
  • Opportunity cost — the value of the option you gave up.
  • AET — the Agricultural Experience Tracker, the electronic record book FFA degree and proficiency applications pull from.
  • Price taker — a producer who must accept the market price. Most commodity producers are.
  • Four Ps — product, price, place, promotion: the skeleton of a marketing plan.

📒 Activity 10.1.1 — The Three Records That Carry the Load

Three documents, three different questions answered:

RecordCoversAnswers
Balance sheetONE date (a snapshot)What am I worth right now?
Income statementA period (a movie)Did I make or lose money?
Cash flow statementA period, month by monthWHEN does money move — will I be short in March?
A student's show-lamb operation on October 1: two lambs worth $800, feed on hand $150, equipment $550 — assets $1,500. Feed-store account owed: $400. Net worth = $1,500 − $400 = $1,100. Solvent? Yes — assets outweigh debts.
  1. Rebuild that balance sheet with one change: the student also owes a $250 entry-fee loan. New net worth? Still solvent? Show the subtraction.
  2. Why can an operation be PROFITABLE on paper and still be unable to pay the feed bill in March? Name the record that catches this problem before it happens.
  3. Depreciation: a $12,000 used tractor will work for ten years. How much expense goes on EACH year's income statement? Why is charging all $12,000 to year one a lie about that year — and a lie about the next nine? And why is the 40 acres it drives on never depreciated?
  4. Sort these into asset or liability: heifer you own · operating loan balance · welder from Lesson 9.2 · unpaid vet bill · 50 bales of hay in the barn · money a neighbor owes YOU for custom work. (One of these fools almost everybody.)
  5. Your SAE, on the record: is a show-pig herd entrepreneurship or placement? The Saturday feed-store job? Which one carries the RISK of losing money — and which one teaches you on someone else's dime? Where does an FFA member keep these records, and what two applications pull straight from those entries?

💵 Activity 10.1.2 — Getting Money In & Keeping Risk Down

Nobody starts with enough cash. The question is borrowing ON PURPOSE — the right loan for the right thing, with the risk managed:

LoanLengthBuys
OperatingUnder a yearFeed, seed, fuel — used up this season
IntermediateA few yearsMachinery, breeding stock
Long-term real estateDecadesLand
Simple interest: a $5,000 operating loan at 8% for one year costs $5,000 × 0.08 = $400 — so the crop it buys inputs for had better clear more than $400.
  1. Match the loan to the purchase, and say why the match matters: a round baler · this spring's fertilizer (Lesson 6.3's 50-lb bags) · 60 acres of pasture. What goes wrong if you buy land with money due back in 12 months?
  2. The five Cs — character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions. A loan officer is reading a 17-year-old's AET records: which TWO Cs do complete, honest records speak to most directly, and what is each C actually asking about you?
  3. Paperwork sort — contract, lease, deed, insurance policy: which one transfers ownership · grants use without ownership · binds two parties to an agreement · trades a premium for someone else carrying the risk? For the insurance one: name a risk from this course (Unit 8's disease outbreak, Unit 9's shop fire...) worth paying a premium against.
  4. The decision process — define the problem, list alternatives, compare costs and benefits, choose, act, evaluate. Run it in writing on a real choice: $600 saved, and the options are a better show animal, a welder like Lesson 9.2's, or leaving it in savings. Name the opportunity cost of YOUR choice — what did you give up?
  5. Three risk tools — diversifying enterprises, cash reserves, forward contracting a price. A hay grower does all three: what does each one protect against? For the forward contract, use the phrase "price taker" and explain what the grower gave up in exchange for certainty.

📣 Activity 10.1.3 — Selling What You Raise

Marketing is NOT advertising. It's finding who wants the product, and delivering it at a price that works — decided BEFORE you produce, not after the barn is full.

Supply and demand, live: a record regional hay crop with flat demand → price falls, and a price taker takes it. That's WHY forward contracts exist — Lesson 6.2's alfalfa grower locking a price before the first cutting.
  1. Eggs at the farmers market vs. corn at the elevator: which producer is a price taker and which one gets to SET a price? What does the egg seller have that the corn seller doesn't?
  2. Build the four Ps for backyard eggs: product (what makes them different?), price (what must it cover — Lesson 7.3's feed math belongs here), place, promotion. Then the fifth piece a real plan adds: how will you MEASURE whether it worked?
  3. Micro or macro? Choosing your lamb's sale price · the Fed raising interest rates · a drought cutting the county hay supply · a trade deal opening a beef market overseas. One sentence each on how the two MACRO items still reach one kid's SAE.
  4. A business plan is bigger than a marketing plan. List the parts a loan officer reads (business description, market analysis, management, product line, marketing strategy, funding request, financial projections) — then say which TWO sections your Activity 10.1.1 records make believable, and why the plan is the document a lender reads FIRST.
Try it at home — your own balance sheet: build a real one-date balance sheet for YOU. Assets: everything you own worth listing (bike, phone, savings, animal, tools) with honest dollar values. Liabilities: anything you owe anyone. Subtract to your net worth. Then write two sentences: which asset is depreciating fastest, and one move that would grow your net worth this year. Nobody else's numbers — the skill is honest valuation, and it's the same sheet a bank will someday ask you for.

📈 Project 10.1.4 — Break-Even: The Number That Decides Everything

One number tells you whether an enterprise deserves to exist: what it COSTS you to produce one unit. Sell above it, you profit. Below it, every sale loses money faster.

Show pig, all-in: pig $150 + feed 600 lb × $0.30/lb = $180 + vet & entry $45 = $375 total cost. Sale weight 280 lb → break-even price = $375 ÷ 280 lb ≈ $1.34 per pound. The market bid is $1.25/lb. Now you KNOW — before the auctioneer does.

Pick ONE enterprise you could genuinely run — eggs, mowing lawns, baked goods, firewood, custom welding, a market animal — and build its break-even worksheet:

  1. Every cost, listed: startup items (depreciated over their working life, per Activity 10.1.1 — the mower isn't free just because you already own it) and per-unit costs (feed, ingredients, fuel, bags).
  2. The unit: what do you sell one OF (a dozen, a lawn, a pound)? How many units per month, honestly?
  3. The math: total cost ÷ units = break-even price. Show it like the pig example.
  4. The market check: what do buyers around you actually pay? Above or below your break-even? By how much?
  5. The decision: run the Activity 10.1.2 decision process on GO or NO-GO — including the opportunity cost of your time. A NO-GO with honest numbers earns full credit; that's the whole point of finding out on paper first.
