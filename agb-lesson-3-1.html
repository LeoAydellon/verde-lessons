Lesson 3.1 — Taking Risk Seriously

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Every business can go wrong in ways the owner doesn't control — bad weather, prices dropping, a loan going bad, a key worker quitting. Smart owners don't just hope; they name the risks ahead of time and make a plan to soften the blow. This lesson teaches the five kinds of risk and how to protect your business against them.

Big idea: you can't remove risk, but you can plan for it so one bad break doesn't sink the whole business.

📖 The five types of risk

  • Production risk — something goes wrong making the product (drought, pests, a sick herd).
  • Market risk — prices move against you (what you sell drops in value, or supplies get more expensive).
  • Financial risk — trouble with money and debt (can't cover a loan payment, run out of cash).
  • Institutional risk — rules and laws change (new regulation, a lost permit, a program ends).
  • Personal risk — something happens to people (injury, illness, a key person leaves).

Two more words you'll use:

  • Cash flow statement — a month-by-month picture of money coming in and going out, so you can spot the tight months.
  • Shortfall / surplus — a shortfall is a month where more goes out than comes in; a surplus is the opposite.

🧮 Activity 3.1.1 — Name That Risk

For each situation, decide which of the five risks it is (production, market, financial, institutional, or personal):

  1. A late frost kills half the strawberry crop.
  2. The price of corn drops right before you sell.
  3. You can't make this month's loan payment.
  4. A new county rule requires an expensive permit for your stand.
  5. Your only delivery driver breaks an arm.
  6. Feed prices double over the winter.
Answer: Pick one of the risks above and describe one thing the owner could do ahead of time to soften it.

A cash flow statement shows money in and out each month. Here's a simple one for a produce stand:

  2. Which months have a shortfall (negative)?
  3. Which month is the strongest surplus?
Answer: Why is it smart to notice a shortfall before it happens instead of when you're out of cash?

🏦 Activity 3.1.3 — Planning for the Tight Months

Look back at your cash flow above. In March you're short and planting season is expensive. You could:

  • Borrow a small short-term loan and pay it back after the strong summer months.
  • Save some of the September surplus ahead of time to cover the slow months.
  1. March is short by $700. If you borrow it in March and pay it back in September, will September's surplus cover it? (Use your numbers.)
  2. Which do you think is smarter here — borrowing or saving ahead? Why?
Answer: How does planning your cash flow help you avoid financial risk?

🛡️ Activity 3.1.4 — Risk Management Strategies

Businesses use a few common tools to lower risk:

  • Diversification — sell more than one thing, so one failure doesn't wipe you out.
  • Insurance — pay a little now to be protected against a big loss later.
  • Value-added products — turn a raw product into something worth more (milk → cheese).
  • Contracts — lock in a price ahead of time so market swings hurt less.
