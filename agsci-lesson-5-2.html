Lesson 5.2 — Heredity & the Molecules of Life

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🐄 What this lesson is about

Why does a calf look like its parents — but never exactly like either one, and never exactly like its brother? In Lesson 5.1 you held DNA in your hand. This lesson is about how that DNA gets DEALT, like cards, from parents to offspring — and why the shuffle is the whole reason breeding works.

Big idea: every plant and animal breeder on Earth is playing the same card game. Meiosis shuffles the deck, a Punnett square tells you the odds, and selection — the thing you learned in Lesson 2.1 — picks the winning hands, generation after generation.

Two divisions, two jobs: mitosis makes 2 identical cells (growth and repair). Meiosis makes 4 different cells with HALF the chromosomes (sex cells) — so that when two halves meet at fertilization, the full number comes back.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Mitosis — one cell splits into two exact copies, full chromosome number. How a seedling grows and a wound heals.
  • Meiosis — one cell makes four gametes (sex cells: pollen, eggs, sperm), each with HALF the chromosomes.
  • Crossing over — during meiosis, paired chromosomes swap matching segments. Every gamete gets a hand of cards neither parent chromosome had.
  • Allele — one version of a gene. Offspring get one allele from each parent.
  • Dominant / recessive — a dominant allele (written T) shows up with just one copy; a recessive trait (written t) only shows when there are two.
  • Homozygous / heterozygous — two matching alleles (TT or tt) vs a mixed pair (Tt).
  • Genotype / phenotype — the letters an organism CARRIES vs the trait you can SEE. A Tt corn plant carries a short allele you'll never spot in the field.
  • Punnett square — the four-box grid that predicts the odds of each offspring combination.
  • Carbohydrates — sugars, starch, cellulose: quick energy and plant structure.
  • Lipids — fats and oils: the MOST energy per gram, and the stuff of membranes.
  • Proteins — chains of amino acids: muscle, structure, and every enzyme.
  • Nucleic acids — DNA and RNA, chains of nucleotides carrying information.

➗ Activity 5.2.1 — Two Divisions, Sorted

Mix-up between mitosis and meiosis is the most common test mistake in this whole standard. Sort it out once, for good:

  1. Sort each phrase under the right division: makes 4 cells · makes 2 cells · half the chromosomes · full chromosome number · identical copies · all different · grows a corn stalk taller · makes the pollen in the tassel · heals a cut · crossing over happens here.
    MitosisMeiosis
  2. Why MUST gametes carry half the chromosome number? What would happen to the count at fertilization if they carried the full set?
  3. A steer's muscle cells and its skin cells carry identical DNA (mitosis made them both). So why do the calves it sires all differ from each other? Name the division — and the shuffle inside it — that makes every gamete unique.
  4. Genetic diversity actually has THREE sources working together: crossing over, chromosomes sorting independently, and which sperm happens to meet which egg. Which of the three, if any, can a rancher control by choosing which two animals to mate?
Breeder's truth: in Lesson 2.1 you learned selection improved crops for 10,000 years. Selection only works if offspring VARY. In one sentence: what would happen to a breeding program if reproduction worked by mitosis — every offspring an exact copy?

🪙 Activity 5.2.2 — The Odds, Two Ways (coins, then the square)

You are the gamete-maker. Grab two coins — each coin is one parent, heads = T (tall), tails = t (short). Both parents are Tt, so a flip is exactly how meiosis "chooses" which allele goes into the gamete:

  • Flip both coins together 20 times. Tally each result: TT, Tt (one head one tail), tt.
  1. Report your 20-flip tally. Now draw the Punnett square for Tt × Tt. What does the SQUARE predict — what fraction TT, Tt, tt?
  2. Corn: tall (T) is dominant over short (t). Out of your square, what percent of offspring LOOK tall, and what percent ARE secretly carrying a short allele? (That's phenotype vs genotype, in one question.)
  3. Did your coin tally match the square's 1:2:1 exactly? Explain why a prediction of odds and a real sample of 20 almost never match perfectly — and what would happen to the match if you flipped 2,000 times. (Unit 3 callback: sample size.)
  4. Cattle: polled (no horns, P) is dominant over horned (p). A rancher wants NO horned calves, ever. One bull is PP, another is Pp — both look identical (polled). Draw Pp × pp and PP × pp squares. Which bull is the safe purchase, and why can't the rancher tell them apart by looking?
Think it through: a recessive trait can hide for generations inside heterozygous parents, then reappear. Explain how two polled cattle can produce a horned calf — where were the two p alleles hiding?

🥣 Activity 5.2.3 — Four Molecules, One Feed Bag

Everything alive — and everything you feed anything alive — is built from four macromolecules. A livestock feed tag is literally a macromolecule report:

  1. Match the molecule to its job: carbohydrates · lipids · proteins · nucleic acids → most energy per gram, builds membranes · quick energy and plant structure · carries the genetic information · amino-acid chains that make muscle and every enzyme.
  2. A feed tag reads: Crude Protein 16% · Crude Fat 4% · Crude Fiber 12%. Name the macromolecule behind each line. (Fiber is mostly cellulose — which of the four is that?)
  3. Why does a lamb being finished for market get a higher-FAT ration, gram for gram, than a maintenance ration? Use "energy per gram" in your answer.
  4. Enzymes digest the feed in the first place — and enzymes are which macromolecule? So a protein shortage in the ration hurts the animal twice. Explain both hits.
  5. Lesson 5.1 callback: which macromolecule did you pull out of the strawberry, and which one broke open when the dish soap hit the cell membranes?
Full-circle: DNA (nucleic acid) codes for proteins, some proteins are enzymes, enzymes unlock energy from carbohydrates and lipids. Write that chain as four arrows — you've just summarized this entire unit in one line.

📋 Project 5.2.4 — Design a Breeding Plan

Be the breeder. Pick ONE real trait in ONE real animal or crop you care about — polled cattle, tall/short corn, coat color in goats or dogs, flower color in a garden plant (look up whether your trait is dominant or recessive; if you can't find it, say so and assign letters as your stated assumption):

  • Define the trait, the letters (like P/p), and which allele is dominant.
  • Choose two parents by genotype and say WHY a breeder would start there.
  • Predict: draw the Punnett square, give the expected genotype fractions AND what each offspring would look like (phenotype).
  • Plan generation two: which offspring would you keep to breed next, and which would you cull from the program? What are you selecting FOR?
  • Be honest about the odds: in one sentence, why might your first four offspring all come out the "wrong" way even if your square is perfect? (Every birth is its own coin flip.)
Honesty rule (same as every unit): if your trait turned out to be controlled by many genes instead of one (most real traits are!), report that and run your plan on the simplified one-gene version anyway — just SAY that's the simplification. Real breeders name their assumptions.
