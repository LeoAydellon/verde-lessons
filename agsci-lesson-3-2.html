Lesson 3.2 — Run Your Own Investigation

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Last lesson you learned the toolkit. This lesson you USE it — start to finish. You'll design an investigation, keep a real science notebook, turn raw numbers into tables and graphs, and say what the data actually shows (which is sometimes "not what I expected" — that counts as a result too).

Big idea: data you didn't write down doesn't exist, and data nobody can read doesn't help. A scientist's real products are the notebook, the table, the graph, and the honest conclusion — that's exactly what you'll make here, and it ends with a real experiment you run yourself.

Design it → run it → record it → graph it → say what it means. That's the whole job.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Procedure — the numbered steps of your experiment, written so someone else could repeat it exactly.
  • Trial / replicate — one run of the experiment. More replicates = more trustworthy results (one lucky seed proves nothing).
  • Science notebook — where observations, sketches, questions, and ideas get recorded AS they happen — dated, in ink, mistakes crossed out not erased.
  • Data table — rows and columns with labeled units, filled in during the experiment, not from memory afterward.
  • Mean (average) — add the values, divide by how many. Smooths out one weird result.
  • Bar graph vs line graph — bars compare groups; lines show change over time.
  • Conclusion — what the data says about your hypothesis: supported, not supported, or can't tell yet.

📐 Activity 3.2.1 — Design It

Your question: "Do radish seeds sprout faster with liquid fertilizer in the water?" Design the investigation by answering these:

  1. Write the hypothesis ("If… then… because…").
  2. Independent variable (the ONE thing you change): ______
  3. Dependent variable (what you measure, with its unit): ______
  4. Name THREE things that must stay the same for every seed (think: light, water amount, temperature, seed type…).
  5. What's your control group, and why do you need it?
  6. Write a numbered procedure (5–8 steps) someone else could follow without asking you anything.
The replicate question: Why should you plant 10 seeds per group instead of 1? One sentence.

📓 Activity 3.2.2 — Table It and Graph It

A student ran that exact experiment — 10 seeds with plain water, 10 with fertilizer water — and scribbled this mess on scratch paper:

"Day 3: plain 2 sprouted, fert 1. Day 4: plain 5, fert 3. Day 5: plain 8, fert 6. Day 6: plain 9, fert 8. Day 7: plain 9, fert 9. Final heights day 10 (cm) — plain: 4, 5, 5, 6, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4 · fert: 6, 7, 6, 8, 7, 6, 7, 7."

  1. Build a clean data table for the sprout counts (columns: Day · Plain water sprouted · Fertilizer sprouted).
  2. Compute the mean final height for each group (round to one decimal).
  3. Which needs a line graph — sprouts over days, or final heights of the two groups? Which needs a bar graph? Sketch BOTH (paper is fine — label both axes and the units).
Look closely: the plain group has 9 heights listed and the fertilizer group has 8 — but 10 seeds each were planted. What probably happened, and how should the notebook record it? (Hint: missing data gets explained, never invented.)

🧠 Activity 3.2.3 — Say What It Means

Using YOUR table and means from 3.2.2, write a short conclusion (4–6 sentences) that answers:

  1. Did fertilizer make seeds sprout faster? What do the day-by-day counts say?
  2. Did fertilizer change how tall the sprouts grew? By how much, on average?
  3. So: was the hypothesis supported, not supported, or partly? (It's allowed to be "partly" — say which part.)
  4. Propose the next investigation: one follow-up question this experiment makes you want to ask.
The honesty rule: your conclusion may only use numbers that are actually in the table. "It seemed healthier" is not data. If you want "healthier" in a conclusion, you have to have measured something.

📋 Project 3.2.4 — The Real Thing (runs about a week)

Now run a real investigation with stuff from home — no lab needed. Question: does light matter for sprouting seeds? (Or propose your own question to your teacher first.)

  • Setup: two plates, each with a folded damp paper towel and 5–10 of the same seeds (dry beans from the kitchen work). One plate somewhere bright, one inside a dark cupboard. Keep both towels equally damp.
  • Every day for 5–7 days: record in your notebook — date, count sprouted, anything you notice, and a quick sketch (stick-figure sprouts are fine; sketches ARE science notes).
  • At the end: data table · one graph · a conclusion using the honesty rule · one follow-up question.
Before you start, predict: write your hypothesis down FIRST, dated. A prediction made after you've seen the results is just a memory wearing a lab coat.

✅ Quick self-check

  1. Why do more replicates make results more trustworthy?
  2. When do you use a line graph, and when a bar graph?
  3. What belongs in a science notebook, and when do you write it?
  4. What are the three honest endings a conclusion can have?
