Unit 8 · Agriscience III

Lesson 8.1 — Integrated Pest Management

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

Everything you've grown or raised in this course has enemies: insects chewing the lettuce, weeds stealing the water (in Arizona, the crime that matters most — Lesson 4.2), worms quietly starving a lamb from the inside (Lesson 7.2), rodents in the feed room. The old answer was "see a bug, grab a jug." Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is the smarter answer: scout first, count, and only act when the numbers say the pest costs more than the fix — then pick from a whole toolbox, not just the chemical shelf.

Big idea: IPM is Lesson 2.3's evidence rule wearing work gloves. You don't spray because it's Tuesday or because you're nervous — you spray (or rotate, or trap, or release lady beetles) because you counted, compared the count to a threshold, and the data said GO.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Pest — any organism that damages crops, livestock, or stored products: insects, weeds, rodents, parasites, disease organisms.
  • Scouting — regularly walking the field or checking the herd and COUNTING pests and damage, so decisions run on numbers instead of nerves.
  • Economic injury level (EIL) — the pest population at which the damage costs as much as the control would.
  • Economic (action) threshold — set a little BELOW the injury level: the count at which you act, so the population never reaches the point of real loss.
  • Cultural control — changing the practice: crop rotation, sanitation, resistant varieties, planting dates, rotating pastures.
  • Mechanical/physical control — removing the pest directly: tillage, hand pulling, mowing, traps, screens, sticky cards.
  • Biological control — hiring the pest's natural enemies: lady beetles, lacewings, parasitic wasps, and Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) on caterpillars.
  • Chemical control — pesticides. Still in the toolbox — but in IPM it comes out when the OTHER tools haven't held the pest below threshold.
  • Resistance — what a pest population evolves when it sees the same chemical over and over (Lesson 5.2's genetics working against you). Rotating chemical classes slows it.
  • Biosecurity — everything done to keep disease OUT of a farm and to keep it from spreading: isolation, footbaths, traffic control, disinfection.
  • Signal word — the toxicity rank on a pesticide label: DANGER–POISON (skull & crossbones, most toxic) → DANGER → WARNING → CAUTION (least).
  • REI (restricted-entry interval) — the wait before workers may re-enter a treated field. PHI (preharvest interval) — the wait between the last application and harvest.
  • Restricted-use pesticide — one that may only be applied by (or under the supervision of) a certified applicator.

🔍 Activity 8.1.1 — Know the Pest by the Damage

You usually meet the DAMAGE before you meet the pest. Learn to read it like tracks:

What you findWho did it
Ragged holes, stripped leavesChewing insects (caterpillars, beetles, grasshoppers)
Stippled, curled, yellowing leaves + sticky "honeydew" turning black with sooty moldSucking insects (aphids, whiteflies, lygus bugs)
Crop thin and thirsty; something ELSE green is thriving between the rowsWeeds — stealing water, light, and nutrients
Droppings, gnawed feed sacks, greasy rub marks along walls, burrowsRodents — and they contaminate far more feed than they eat
Weight loss on good feed, rough coat, scours; pale eyelids and "bottle jaw"Internal parasites (the barber pole worm's anemia — Lesson 7.2)
Rubbing, hair loss, restless cattle bunching up, lower gainsExternal parasites (horn flies, ticks, lice, mange mites)
  1. Sucking insects don't leave holes — explain what they're actually doing to the leaf, using Lesson 6.1's plumbing words (which tube are they tapping: xylem or phloem, and what's flowing in it?). Why does that explain the sticky honeydew?
  2. Why do weeds hurt an Arizona field MORE than the same weeds hurt a field in rainy Ohio? (One sentence — Lesson 4.2 already gave you the answer.)
  3. A ewe is losing weight on good feed, and her lower eyelids are pale instead of pink. Name the likely pest family, what "bottle jaw" is, and WHY pale eyelids mean anemia (what is the barber pole worm drinking?).
  4. The feed-room clue set: droppings + gnawed sacks + greasy rub marks. Beyond the feed they eat, name TWO other costs rodents put on a farm (think Lesson 8.2's coming food-safety rules, and think wiring).

🧰 Activity 8.1.2 — The IPM Toolbox: Four Drawers

IPM combines methods. Four drawers, opened in roughly this order — chemical LAST, not first:

Cultural (change the practice) → Mechanical/Physical (remove the pest directly) → Biological (hire its enemies) → Chemical (when scouting says the others haven't held the line)
  1. Sort these twelve tools into the four drawers: crop rotation · sticky cards · lady beetles · a labeled aphid spray · tillage · resistant seed varieties · parasitic wasps · window screens on the poultry house · planting two weeks earlier to dodge a pest's peak · Bt on caterpillars · mousetraps · rotating cattle to a fresh pasture.
  2. Pasture rotation appears in BOTH Unit 7 and this unit. Explain how moving the herd breaks an internal parasite's life cycle (where do the worm's eggs and larvae wait, and what happens to them if no host shows up?). Which drawer is this tool in?
  3. Crop rotation vs. the corn rootworm: the rootworm's eggs overwinter in a cornfield's soil expecting corn again next spring. What happens to the hatchlings if the field is beans next year — and why does this cost the farmer almost nothing? (Lesson 6.3 gave you a SECOND reason to rotate corn with beans — name it for a bonus: think Rhizobium.)
  4. Biological control is a hire, not a purchase-and-forget: why would spraying a broad insecticide the week after releasing lady beetles waste both purchases?
  5. Resistance: using Lesson 5.2's genetics, explain in 2–3 sentences how a population of aphids "learns" to survive a chemical nobody changed. (Hint: nobody learns anything — who survives, and who are the next generation's parents?) What's the IPM habit that slows this down?

➗ Activity 8.1.3 — Is It Worth Treating? The Numbers Decide

Scouting gives you a count. The count meets two lines:

Economic injury level (EIL): the population where damage cost = control cost.
Economic threshold (ET): set a little LOWER — the "act now" line, so the population never climbs to the EIL. Below the threshold, spraying costs more than the pest does.
  1. Draw (or describe) the two lines on a pest-population graph, threshold below injury level. Why is the threshold set below the injury level instead of AT it? (What is a pest population doing while your sprayer is still hooked to the tractor?)
  2. Run the numbers: your scouting says aphids are costing an alfalfa field about $18/acre in lost yield at the current count. The spray + fuel + your time costs $30/acre. Treat or wait — and what would have to change to flip the answer?
  3. Same field, two weeks later: the count has tripled and damage is tracking toward $55/acre. Now what — and which drawer(s) do you open first if a chemical is justified? (Trick: it can be "chemical, AND a cultural change so next year never gets here.")
  4. Pests cost a producer four ways. Name all four (lost ____, lower ____, reduced ____ ____ and milk, and the cost of ____ itself) and give a livestock example of the third one from Lesson 7.2's horn flies.
  5. One sentence: why is "spray every two weeks all summer, just in case" BOTH bad economics and a resistance factory? (Two answers from this lesson meet in that one habit.)

🚧 Activity 8.1.4 — Biosecurity: Keeping Disease Off the Farm

The cheapest disease is the one that never arrives. Biosecurity is the whole set of habits that keep it out — and the county fair is the classic test of it:

  1. The show-animal problem: your market lamb just spent five days at the fair breathing the same barn air as two hundred strange sheep, then comes home to your flock. Write the coming-home protocol: how long is the standard isolation (about ____ days), WHERE does the lamb live during it, and name two things you do between chores on the isolated lamb and chores on the main flock.
  2. Sort these into the farm biosecurity kit and say what each one blocks: footbath at the barn door · visitor log and a "check in first" sign · disinfecting the borrowed trailer before AND after · rodent and bird control in the feed room · vaccinations on a calendar · proper disposal of dead stock. (Two of these connect straight back to earlier lessons — 7.3's vaccine calendar and this lesson's rodent damage. Say which.)
  3. Zoom out — beyond your fence, disease control has layers. Match the level to the tool: state/national: brand inspection · health certificates for interstate movement · USDA APHIS traceability and global: import quarantine · international disease reporting. Why does a health certificate for ONE trailer of heifers crossing a state line protect ranchers who never meet those heifers?
  4. Foot-and-mouth disease and avian influenza are the nightmare names. Pick one and answer: why does a SINGLE confirmed case trigger a response that looks wildly out of proportion (movement freezes, whole-flock culls)? Use the words "spreads" and "export" in your answer.
  5. Design check: name the biosecurity habit YOUR family already practices without calling it that (washing hands after handling the chickens counts; so does not letting the dog in the coop). What is it blocking?

⚠️ Activity 8.1.5 — The Label Is the Law

A pesticide label isn't advice — it's a legal document. Using the product any way the label prohibits is a violation of federal law. Here's a mock label; read it like an inspector:

APHID-HALT EC  ·  Active ingredient: flonicamid 17%
EPA Reg. No. 12345-678  ·  Signal word: WARNING
PPE: long sleeves, chemical-resistant gloves, eye protection
Restricted-entry interval (REI): 12 hours  ·  Preharvest interval (PHI), lettuce: 3 days
Rate: 2.8 fl oz per acre. Do not apply when wind exceeds 10 mph. Triple-rinse container into the tank. Store in original container, locked.
  1. Rank the four signal words from most to least toxic, and place APHID-HALT on that ladder. Which signal word carries the skull and crossbones?
  2. REI vs. PHI — two different clocks protecting two different groups of people. Whom does each one protect, and for this label: when may your crew re-enter, and what's the earliest you may cut the lettuce after spraying?
  3. Your neighbor says "double the rate, kill 'em twice as dead." Give THREE separate reasons that's wrong — one legal, one financial, one from Activity 8.1.2's resistance story. (Bonus: a residue reason that Lesson 8.2 will care about.)
  4. Why does the sprayer get CALIBRATED before application (what two mistakes does calibration prevent — one that wastes money, one that breaks the law)?
  5. Walk the safe-application checklist in order: read the ____ first → put on the listed ____ → ____ the sprayer so the rate is exact → mix in a ____ area → check the ____ so drift doesn't leave your field → afterwards, ____-rinse the container into the tank → store chemicals ____ in their ____ containers → write down what, where, when, and how much.
  6. Who may apply a restricted-use pesticide? And connect it: which agency registers pesticides and issued that Reg. No.? (Lesson 8.2 introduces the full agency cast — this is your first entry.)

🪤 Activity 8.1.6 — Real Scouting: Run a Sticky-Card Survey (at home)

Professionals scout with yellow sticky cards — flying pests can't resist the color and can't leave. Build one and run a real 3-day survey:

  1. Build: cut a bright YELLOW card about the size of a postcard (yellow paper, or white paper colored solid yellow). Coat one side thinly with petroleum jelly. Tape or clothespin it, sticky side out, beside a houseplant, garden plant, porch light, or the chicken run. (Ask first; keep it where pets won't nose it.)
  2. Scout: once a day for 3 days, count what's stuck WITHOUT touching it. Record a data table (Lesson 3.2 rules): date, location, total insects, and your best sort into categories — tiny flies · aphid-sized · beetles · other.
  3. Decide like a producer: invent a reasonable action threshold for your location (for example, "more than 10 aphid-sized insects per card per day means I'd act"). Did your count cross it? Write the treat-or-wait call and your reasoning, exactly like Activity 8.1.3.
  4. Classify your own tool: which IPM drawer is a sticky card in? Could it be BOTH a scouting tool and a control? Explain.
No good spot for a card? Alternate survey: pick any outdoor plant and inspect 10 leaves top and bottom (a real scouting pattern). Record damage types using the Activity 8.1.1 table — holes vs. stippling vs. nothing — and write the same treat-or-wait call from your evidence.

📋 Project 8.1.7 — The IPM Plan

Write a one-page IPM plan for ONE pest problem — pick a plant pest (aphids on lettuce, weeds in a garden bed) or an animal parasite (worms in a ewe flock, horn flies on cattle):

  • The pest and its evidence: what damage announces it (Activity 8.1.1), and how you'll scout for it — what you count, how often.
  • The threshold: a stated action threshold with a sentence of reasoning (real published thresholds exist for many pests — cite one if you can find it; if sources disagree, give the range and say whose number you trust, the same honesty rule as every unit).
  • The toolbox: at least ONE tool from EACH of the four drawers, in the order you'd deploy them — and which single tool you'd bet on most for THIS pest, with why.
  • The chemical page: IF the threshold trips — the signal word you'd hope to find on the mildest effective product, the PPE you'd wear, and the two clocks (REI/PHI or, for livestock, the withdrawal time from Lesson 7.3) you'd write on the calendar before anything else.
  • Next year: one cultural change that makes this whole plan less necessary next season.
