Unit 9 · Agriscience III

Lesson 9.1 — Planning & Building Ag Structures

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

For eight units you've been learning what happens ON the land — plants, animals, water, pests. This unit is about the land itself and everything you bolt to it. Before a single fence post goes in, somebody has to answer four very unglamorous questions: WHERE exactly is the property line? Is the ground actually level? What will this cost in boards and bags of concrete? And will it still be standing after ten monsoon seasons? Every one of those questions has real math behind it — the kind where a wrong answer costs actual money or lets actual cattle out onto the highway.

Big idea: a structure is a set of decisions made on paper BEFORE anyone picks up a hammer. The sketch, the survey, and the bill of materials are where a build succeeds or fails — the construction just makes the paperwork visible.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Public Land Survey System (PLSS) — the grid Arizona land is legally described in, measured from the Gila and Salt River Baseline & Meridian.
  • Township — a 6-mile × 6-mile square of that grid, holding 36 sections.
  • Section — one square mile = 640 acres. Numbered 1–36 starting in the northeast corner, snaking west, then back east, row by row.
  • Metes and bounds — the older system: walk the boundary by compass bearings and distances, and the description must close back on the point of beginning.
  • Lot-and-block — the system for town lots and subdivisions (your house probably has one).
  • Benchmark — a point of KNOWN elevation that a survey starts from.
  • Backsight / foresight — rod readings in differential leveling: the backsight looks BACK at the known point, the foresight looks FORWARD to the new one.
  • Height of instrument — benchmark elevation + backsight: the elevation of the level's line of sight.
  • 3-4-5 rule — 3 ft one way, 4 ft the other; if the diagonal is exactly 5 ft, the corner is a true right angle.
  • Plan / elevation / section views — looking straight down · looking at a side · the structure cut open. A common scale: 1/4 inch = 1 foot.
  • Bill of materials — every item, size, quantity, unit price, total — plus about 10% for waste.
  • Board foot — lumber's unit of volume: (thickness" × width" × length') ÷ 12.
  • Hydration — the chemical reaction that hardens concrete. Concrete CURES, it doesn't dry — which is why you keep it damp.
  • H-brace — the H-shaped post assembly at fence corners and gates, where all the wire's tension actually lives.

🗺 Activity 9.1.1 — Where the Land Legally Is

A deed never says "the field behind the Circle K." It says something like the NE 1/4 of the SW 1/4 of Section 12 — and you read it BACKWARDS, smallest piece last-named, biggest first:

Start with the section: 640 acres.
Take the SW 1/4 of it: 640 ÷ 4 = 160 acres.
Take the NE 1/4 of THAT: 160 ÷ 4 = 40 acres. Done — you've found the forty.
  1. How many acres in a full section? How many sections in a township, and what are a township's outside dimensions?
  2. Work one yourself, showing each division: how many acres is the SE 1/4 of the NE 1/4 of Section 30? And the S 1/2 of the SW 1/4?
  3. Section numbering snakes: 1 starts in the NORTHEAST corner and runs west, then row two runs back east. So which section sits directly SOUTH of Section 6? (Sketch a 6×6 grid and number the first two rows — it's the only way to see it.)
  4. Arizona's PLSS measurements start at the Gila and Salt River Baseline & Meridian. Why does a survey system need ONE fixed starting point that everyone agrees on? (Lesson 3.1's measurement brain: what happens to data when two people use two different zeros?)
  5. Match the system to the property: a 1912 ranch whose deed walks the boundary creek by compass bearings · a new subdivision lot in Maricopa · the school farm's 40 acres. One clue: a metes-and-bounds walk that does NOT close back on its point of beginning is a lawsuit waiting to happen — why?

📐 Activity 9.1.2 — Surveying: Is the Ground Where You Think It Is?

Ground lies. A pad that looks flat can hide a foot of fall — and water always finds it (Lesson 4.2 taught you water never forgets which way is downhill). Differential leveling catches it with simple addition and subtraction:

Height of instrument (HI) = benchmark elevation + backsight
New point's elevation = HI − foresight
Percent slope = (rise ÷ run) × 100
  1. A benchmark sits at 1,250.0 ft. The rod on the benchmark reads 4.2 ft (backsight). The rod on the proposed barn corner reads 6.7 ft (foresight). What's the height of instrument, and what's the barn corner's elevation? Show both steps.
  2. Is that corner higher or lower than the benchmark — and by how much? Explain how you can tell from the two rod readings alone, before doing any math.
  3. A corral site drops 3 ft over a 150-ft run. What's the percent slope? A facility floor wants a gentle slope so it drains (you'll design one in Activity 9.1.5) — but is 2% a lot to stand on? Compare it to a wheelchair ramp or your driveway.
  4. You stake a shed corner: 3 ft down one string, 4 ft down the other, and your tape reads the diagonal at 5 ft 2 in. Is the corner square? Which way do you nudge the stake, and what's the final check on the whole rectangle once all four corners are staked? (Both diagonals must ___.)
  5. An RTK GPS receiver can do all of this in minutes. So why does this lesson still make you learn the rod-and-level math? (Think about Lesson 2.3's evidence rule — what does it mean to be able to CHECK a machine's answer?)
Try it at home — square a real corner with string and a tape: pick any corner you can measure (a garden bed, a patio slab, a room, or two strings staked in the yard). Measure 3 ft from the corner down one edge and mark it; 4 ft down the other edge and mark it; then measure the straight line between your two marks. Exactly 5 ft = a true right angle. Record your diagonal to the nearest 1/4 inch, say whether the corner is square, and — if it's off — which direction the edge would have to swing to fix it. Doubled tape (6-8-10) works too and is more accurate; say so if you used it.

✏️ Activity 9.1.3 — Sketch It, Draw It, Price It

A rough sketch becomes a scaled working drawing, and the drawing becomes money:

ViewWhat it shows
Plan viewLooking straight DOWN — the footprint and layout
Elevation viewA side, face-on — heights, doors, rooflines
Section viewThe structure CUT OPEN — what's inside a wall or footing
Board feet = (thickness" × width" × length') ÷ 12
Concrete (cu yd) = (length' × width' × thickness') ÷ 27
Bill of materials total = every line item + ~10% waste
  1. At 1/4 inch = 1 foot, how long is a 32-ft barn wall on the drawing? And if a drawn feed room measures 2.5 inches, how wide is the real room?
  2. Board-feet practice, showing the formula each time: (a) one 2×6, 12 ft long. (b) ten 2×4s, 8 ft each. (c) a 4×4 post, 10 ft. (Use nominal sizes — that's how lumber math is done.)
  3. A shed slab is 12 ft × 16 ft × 4 inches thick. Careful — convert 4 inches to feet FIRST. How many cubic feet? How many cubic yards? If ready-mix sells by the half yard, what do you order?
  4. Build a mini bill of materials for one 8-ft H-brace assembly: two 8-ft posts, one 8-ft horizontal rail, brace wire, and staples. Invent reasonable unit prices, total it, then add the 10% waste line. Why does every real estimate carry that line — what does Lesson 2.1's history of farm projects tell you about how builds actually go?
  5. Why does the bank, the county permit office, and your future self all want the DRAWING and not just "a barn, about yea big"? One sentence each.

🔨 Activity 9.1.4 — Building It: Concrete, Lumber, Wire & Fence

The build sequence: prep the site → stake the layout with batter boards and string → footings on solid, compacted ground → then up. The materials each have one non-negotiable rule:

MaterialThe ruleWhy
ConcreteKeep it DAMP while curing (common hand mix 1 cement : 2 sand : 3 gravel) It hardens by hydration — a chemical reaction that needs water, not evaporation
LumberPressure-treated anywhere wood touches soil Ground contact = moisture + organisms that eat untreated wood (Unit 5's decomposers, off the clock)
WiringBlack = hot · white = neutral · green/bare = ground; SMALLER gauge number = BIGGER wire Color code is a life-safety language; wire size must match the load
FenceCorners and gates get an H-brace All the wire's tension loads at the ENDS — an unbraced corner post slowly leans and the whole fence goes slack
  1. A crew pours a slab on a 105° June day and knocks off for the weekend. Monday it's covered in surface cracks. Using the word "hydration," explain what went wrong and name two things they should have done. Why is Arizona the hardest state in the country to cure concrete in?
  2. State the 1-2-3 hand mix from memory. Which ingredient is the GLUE, and which two are the filler that gives it body?
  3. Your gate sags and the fence wire has gone slack along the whole run — but every line post stands straight. Where's the actual failure, and what does the H in H-brace physically do with the tension? Sketch it.
  4. A 14-gauge extension cord and a 10-gauge cord are on the shop wall. Which is the heavier wire? Which one runs the welder? And what does it mean that the green wire "does nothing" on a good day — what day was it installed for?
  5. Why pressure-treated lumber for the bottom fence rail but ordinary lumber for the roof rafters? Same tree, same board — what's different about the JOB site?

🐄 Activity 9.1.5 — Design the Facility Around the Animal

Here's where Unit 7 walks into Unit 9. You already know how cattle see and move — the flight zone, the point of balance, the blind spot straight behind (Lesson 7.2). Good facility design is that knowledge poured in concrete: curved lanes with solid sides move cattle calmly (they think they're circling back to where they came from, and solid sides delete the scary flapping shadows), floors slope to drain, and ventilation carries off moisture, ammonia, and heat.

  1. Explain the curved solid-sided lane using Lesson 7.2's vocabulary: what does the curve do with the animal's desire to return, and what does the solid side do to the flight-zone triggers?
  2. Why does a working corral floor slope — and using your Activity 9.1.2 math, how many INCHES of fall does a 2% slope put across a 40-ft pen? Show the calculation.
  3. Ammonia in a closed barn comes from where (Unit 7's digestion knowledge)? Name two things ventilation is removing besides smell, and what each one does to animal health if it stays.
  4. Design review: a plan-view drawing shows a loading chute with a hard 90° corner, see-through rail sides, and the handler walking directly behind the cattle. Find all three mistakes and fix each with one sentence.
  5. The maintenance walk: after a monsoon, list four specific checks from this lesson (fence line, hinges, water lines before a freeze, GFCI test) and add ONE of your own for a facility with animals in it. Why does the lesson say maintenance is "scheduled, not accidental" — and why does writing repairs down matter? (Unit 8 taught you what records are worth.)

🏗 Project 9.1.6 — Plan a Real Structure, Start to Finish (at home)

Pick one small, real structure your place (or an imagined 4-H setup) could actually use — a chicken coop, a hay shelter, a dog kennel, a garden shed, a shade ramada. Then do the WHOLE paper process from this lesson on one or two pages:

  1. Site: where does it go and why — drainage, sun, distance to water/power. One sentence on what the ground does there when it rains hard.
  2. Plan view, to scale: draw it at 1/4 inch = 1 foot (graph paper or neat freehand with a ruler). Label the real dimensions on the drawing.
  3. One more view: add an elevation OR a section view — your choice, labeled.
  4. Bill of materials: at least six line items with sizes, quantities, invented-but-reasonable unit prices, a board-feet calculation for at least one lumber line, and the 10% waste line. Total it.
  5. If it holds animals: one design decision made FOR the animal (Activity 9.1.5). If it doesn't: one design decision made for the monsoon.
  6. Maintenance card: three scheduled checks with their season or interval.
Reality bonus: price ONE line of your bill of materials against a real current price (a store website or a flyer) and note how far off your guess was. Estimators get good by checking themselves — that's Lesson 3.2's feedback loop wearing a tool belt.
