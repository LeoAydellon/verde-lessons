Lesson 2.2 — The Cost of Doing Business

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Last lesson you sorted money into income and expenses. But some costs are sneaky — they don't show up as a bill you pay this month. A tractor slowly wears out. A loan quietly charges you extra. Even your own time is worth money. This lesson is about finding all the real costs of running a business, so you can tell if you're truly making money.

Big idea: a business is only worth doing if what comes in is bigger than everything it really costs — including the hidden costs.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Depreciation — the value a big item (like a tractor) loses each year as it wears out. It's a real cost even though you don't pay a bill for it.
  • Salvage value — what an item is still worth at the end of its useful life (what you could sell it for as scrap or used).
  • Straight-line depreciation — the simplest way to spread that loss out evenly over the years.
  • Principal — the actual amount of money you borrowed on a loan.
  • Interest — the extra you pay the lender for borrowing. It's the price of the loan.
  • Net income — income minus all expenses. What you actually made.
  • Return on assets (ROA) — how good your business is at turning what it owns into profit, shown as a percent.
  • Break-even — the point where income exactly equals expenses. You're not losing money, but not making any yet either.

🧮 Activity 2.2.1 — Straight-Line Depreciation

When a business buys something expensive that lasts several years, it spreads the cost out. Here's the simple formula:

Yearly depreciation = (Cost − Salvage value) ÷ Years of useful life

Example: A tractor costs $30,000. After 10 years you could still sell it for $5,000. So: ($30,000 − $5,000) ÷ 10 = $2,500 lost every year.

💵 Activity 2.2.2 — The Cost of Money (Interest)

Borrowing money isn't free. The extra you pay is interest. For one year, simple interest is:

Interest = Principal × Rate  (rate is written as a decimal — 6% = 0.06)

Example: You borrow $5,000 at 6% for one year → $5,000 × 0.06 = $300 in interest. You'd pay back $5,300 total.

📊 Activity 2.2.3 — Profit or Loss? (Net Income & ROA)

Now put it together. First, net income:

Net income = Income − All expenses (don't forget depreciation and interest!)

Then, how hard your stuff is working for you — return on assets:

ROA = Net income ÷ Total assets × 100 (this gives a percent)

Work this one out: A small farm stand made $20,000 in sales this year. Its expenses were: supplies $9,000, interest $600, depreciation $2,400. It owns $40,000 in assets (land, cooler, truck).

  1. Add up the total expenses.
  2. Find the net income (sales − total expenses).
  3. Did it make a profit or a loss?
  4. Find the ROA (net income ÷ $40,000 × 100).
Answer: If another business had the same net income but only owned $20,000 in assets, would its ROA be higher or lower? What does that tell you about which business is using its stuff more efficiently?

⏰ Activity 2.2.4 — What Is Your Time Worth?

When you run your own business, your labor is a real cost — even if you don't pay yourself a paycheck. If you could earn $15/hour at another job, then every hour you spend on your business "costs" you that $15 you gave up.

Figure it out:

  1. You spend 8 hours a week on your business. What is that worth per week at $15/hour?
  2. What is it worth over a whole year (52 weeks)?
  3. If your business only made $4,000 profit this year, was your time well paid? Why or why not?
Answer: Why should a business owner count their own time as a cost when deciding if a business is really worth it?

⚖️ Activity 2.2.5 — Breaking Even

Break-even is the moment income exactly covers expenses — not a penny of profit, but no loss either. To find how many units you must sell to break even:

Break-even units = Total fixed costs ÷ (Price per unit − Cost to make one unit)

Example: Fixed costs are $600. You sell jars of honey for $8 each, and each jar costs you $3 to produce. That's $5 of profit per jar. $600 ÷ $5 = 120 jars to break even.

Now you:

  1. Fixed costs $900. You sell wreaths for $25, each costs $10 to make. How many wreaths to break even?
  2. After you pass break-even, how much do you earn on every extra wreath you sell?
  3. If you can only make 50 wreaths this season, will you break even? What would you change?
Answer: Why is knowing your break-even point important before you start selling?
