Lesson 2.1 — From Horse to Harvester: How Technology Changed Farming

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

A little over a hundred years ago, most American families farmed — and a farmer with a horse and plow could feed only a handful of people. Today, less than 2% of Americans farm, and one farm can feed more than 150 people. What changed? Technology — wave after wave of it.

Big idea: a few key advances — machines, better seed, selective breeding, and smarter use of resources — let far fewer farmers grow far more food. Every one of those advances changed society, not just farms.

Fewer farmers + better technology = more food. That's why most people today are free to do jobs that aren't farming.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Mechanization — replacing muscle power (human or animal) with machines, like the tractor replacing the horse.
  • Selective breeding — choosing the best plants or animals to reproduce, over many generations. (Humans have done this for thousands of years.)
  • Hybrid seed — seed from crossing two chosen parent plants to get a stronger, higher-yielding crop.
  • Genetic modification (GM) — directly changing an organism's DNA in a lab, often by adding a gene it couldn't get through breeding.
  • Biotechnology — using living things (and their DNA) to make useful products — GM crops, but also things like cheese cultures and medicines.
  • Yield — how much a crop produces per acre.
  • Resource management — using water, soil, and nutrients carefully so they keep producing year after year.

🧬 Activity 2.1.2 — Breeding or Modification?

Selective breeding and genetic modification both change plants and animals — but in different ways. Label each example SB (selective breeding) or GM (genetic modification):

  1. For generations, ranchers keep only the calves from their gentlest, fastest-growing cows.
  2. Scientists add a bacteria gene to corn so the corn makes its own protection against certain insects.
  3. Over hundreds of years, wild mustard is bred into cabbage, broccoli, and kale.
  4. A papaya is given a gene in a lab that makes it resistant to a virus that was wiping out the crop.
  5. A dog breeder pairs two herding dogs hoping for pups with strong herding instinct.
Explain the difference in your own words: what can genetic modification do that selective breeding can't? What do they have in common?

📋 Project 2.1.3 — One Crop, Two Sides

Genetically modified crops are grown on millions of acres — and people still debate them. Here are real points from each side:

  • Reported benefits: higher yields, fewer insect losses, less spraying for some pests, crops that survive drought or disease (like the virus-resistant papaya).
  • Common concerns: weeds and insects can adapt over time, seeds can cost more, some consumers want more labeling and long-term study.

Write two short paragraphs: one explaining the strongest point for GM crops, and one explaining the strongest point of concern. Finish with one sentence: what evidence would help you decide?

Bonus (optional): Ask a parent, grandparent, or neighbor how food or farming changed during their lifetime. Write down the best thing they tell you.
