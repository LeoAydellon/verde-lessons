Lesson 4.2 — Water: Agriculture's Most Precious Input

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Back in Lesson 2.2 you met irrigation and learned what happens when water and soil are managed badly. Now you take the manager's chair. In Arizona, agriculture uses roughly 70% of the water supply — so nobody's choices about water matter more than a farmer's.

Big idea: the question is never just "how much water do we have?" It's "how much of the water we apply actually reaches the crop?" The gap between those two numbers is where a water manager earns their keep.

Same water, more crop. Every technique in this lesson does one thing: close the gap between water applied and water used.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Municipal / industrial / agricultural use — the three big water users: homes and businesses · factories, cooling and mining · irrigation and livestock. Agriculture is the largest.
  • Surface water — rivers and lakes. Arizona's biggest source is the Colorado River, delivered across the state by the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal.
  • Groundwater — water pumped from wells out of aquifers (you met aquifers in 2.2).
  • Reclaimed water (effluent) — treated wastewater from cities, reused for irrigation.
  • Irrigation efficiency — the share of applied water the crop actually gets. Flood ≈ 50% · sprinkler = better · drip = 90%+.
  • Laser leveling — grading a field dead-flat with laser guidance so flood water spreads evenly instead of pooling and running off.
  • Tailwater recovery — a pond at the field's low end that catches runoff so it can be pumped back and used again.
  • Aquaculture — farming fish and shrimp in tanks and ponds.
  • Aquaponics — fish + plants in one loop: bacteria turn fish-waste ammonia into nitrate, plants take up the nitrate, clean water returns to the fish.
  • Brackish — water too salty for crops but usable for some fish. Arizona has warm brackish groundwater doing exactly that job.

🗺 Activity 4.2.1 — Who Gets the Water?

Every drop in Arizona goes to one of three users — municipal, industrial, or agricultural — and comes from one of three sources — surface water, groundwater, or reclaimed water.

  1. Sort these into the three USER groups: a cotton field · a copper mine's cooling system · a Phoenix neighborhood · a feedlot's stock tanks · a data center · a school's drinking fountains.
  2. Agriculture uses about 70% of Arizona's water. Some people hear that and say "farms are wasting our water." Write a fairer two-sentence response — what does that 70% actually produce, and what would happen to food prices if it disappeared?
  3. Name Arizona's three water SOURCES, and for each, one weakness (a canal can be cut by drought upstream… a well can…, reclaimed water depends on…).
  4. The CAP canal moves Colorado River water hundreds of miles across the desert. Why would a state build something that expensive instead of just drilling more wells?
Think it through: pumping groundwater faster than rain refills the aquifer is like spending from a savings account with no deposits. What happens to wells — and to the farms that depend on them — if everyone does it for 30 years?

💧 Activity 4.2.2 — The Efficiency Ladder

Three fields, same crop, each needing the crop to receive 2 acre-feet of water this season:

FieldMethodEfficiencyWater you must APPLY to deliver 2 acre-feet
AFlood50%
BSprinkler75%
CDrip90%
  1. Fill in the last column. (Hint: applied = needed ÷ efficiency. Field A is 2 ÷ 0.50 = 4 acre-feet.)
  2. How many acre-feet does Field C save compared to Field A?
  3. If drip is so efficient, why doesn't every farm use it? Give two honest reasons (think cost, crop type, and what a drip line would go through in a 100-acre wheat field).
  4. Where does the "lost" water in flood irrigation actually go? Name two places.
Manager's math: water in Arizona is bought, pumped, or fought over by the acre-foot. Saving 2 acre-feet per field across 50 fields is 100 acre-feet — a number worth real money. This is why efficiency upgrades pay for themselves.

🔧 Activity 4.2.3 — Stretch Every Drop

Match each problem to the fix that solves it — laser leveling · soil-moisture sensors · lined canals · tailwater recovery pond · recycled dairy flush water — and say WHY it works:

ProblemFixWhy it works
Flood water pools at one end of a bumpy field and runs off the other
A dirt delivery ditch loses water into the ground before it reaches the field
Runoff leaves the low end of the field and is gone for good
The farmer irrigates on a calendar schedule, even when the root zone is still wet
A dairy uses thousands of gallons a day washing out the barn — once
Notice the pattern: not one of these fixes finds NEW water. Every one of them reuses, keeps, or better-aims water the farm already had. In a desert, that's usually the only water you're going to get.

🐟 Activity 4.2.4 — Farming IN Water

Water isn't only an input — sometimes it's the field itself.

  1. Arizona has warm groundwater too salty for crops. Aquaculture turns that "useless" water into shrimp and fish farms. Why is this a smart use of a resource no one else wanted?
  2. Trace the aquaponics loop and fill in the blanks: fish release ammonia → bacteria convert it to ______ → plants absorb it as fertilizer → the water returns to the fish ______.
  3. In aquaponics, what would happen to the fish if you removed the plants and bacteria? What would happen to the plants if you removed the fish? (Two sentences — this loop is a tiny ecosystem.)
  4. Vertical farms stack crops indoors under LEDs and recycle almost all their water — but they "pay for every photon." What does that phrase mean, and which cost did the sun cover for free outdoors?
Connect it: aquaponics is the 4.1 energy lesson's food-chain idea running in a circle on purpose — waste from one organism becomes food for the next, with bacteria as the decomposers. Nothing leaves the loop.

📋 Project 4.2.5 — The Water Audit

You're auditing Rio Verde Farms: 200 acres of flood-irrigated alfalfa (crop needs 2 acre-feet per acre per season), unlined dirt ditches, no tailwater pond, irrigation on a fixed calendar. Water comes from one well that's been dropping for years. Write the audit:

  • The bill today: at 50% flood efficiency, how many acre-feet must the farm apply per acre — and for all 200 acres?
  • Recommend three upgrades from this lesson. For each: what it does, roughly what it saves, and which you'd do FIRST with a limited budget.
  • The well question: explain to the owner, in plain words, why the dropping well makes these upgrades urgent instead of optional.
  • Stretch goal: the farm has one low corner with salty groundwater seeping up. Propose one money-making use for it from this lesson.
Audit rule: every recommendation needs a number or a mechanism. "Use less water" is a wish; "line the main ditch so delivery losses stop before the field" is a fix.
