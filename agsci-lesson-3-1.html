Lesson 3.1 — The Scientific Process on the Farm

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Every farmer runs experiments, whether they call them that or not. "Grandpa always planted after the last frost" started as somebody noticing, trying, and remembering. The difference between a guess and knowledge is a process: ask, predict, test, measure, conclude.

Big idea: agriculture runs on science. Universities test seed varieties in field trials, ranchers track which feed puts on weight, and you can use the exact same process on a windowsill. This lesson is the toolkit: the methods researchers use, how to write a testable hypothesis, how to pick sources you can trust — and how to work safely while you do it.

"I think" → "I tested" → "I know (for now)." Science is the road between those three.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Scientific process — observe → question → hypothesize → experiment → collect data → conclude → share.
  • Experiment / field trial — changing ONE thing on purpose and measuring what happens (a field trial is an experiment out on real land).
  • Survey — asking many people (or checking many farms) the same questions and counting the answers.
  • Observational study — watching and recording without changing anything (like counting pollinators visiting a field).
  • Hypothesis — a testable prediction, usually "If ___, then ___, because ___."
  • Variable — anything that can change in an experiment. The independent variable is what YOU change; the dependent variable is what you measure; everything else should stay the same.
  • Control — the comparison group where you change nothing, so you can see what your change actually did.
  • Credible source — one with expertise and evidence behind it, and nothing to sell you.

🔬 Activity 3.1.1 — Which Method Fits?

For each research job, write which method fits best: experiment/field trial, survey, or observational study.

  1. Find out whether a new drought-tolerant wheat really yields more than the old variety in this county.
  2. Find out how many ranchers in Arizona are already using solar-powered well pumps.
  3. Find out what times of day deer are getting into the orchard.
  4. Find out whether shade cloth changes how fast lettuce bolts in summer heat.
  5. Find out how customers at a farmers market feel about paying more for organic eggs.
Then answer: Pick ONE of the jobs above. What would be hard about getting a trustworthy answer, and how would you handle it? One or two sentences.

💡 Activity 3.1.2 — Build a Hypothesis

Turn each farm question into a full hypothesis using the frame "If ___, then ___, because ___." Then name the independent variable (what you'd change) and the dependent variable (what you'd measure).

  1. Does soaking seeds overnight make them sprout faster?
  2. Do hens lay more eggs with more hours of light?
  3. Does compost improve how much water sandy soil holds?

Example: "If tomato plants get fish-emulsion fertilizer weekly, then they will produce more tomatoes by weight, because the extra nitrogen supports more growth." Change: fertilizer. Measure: tomato weight.

The test question: a hypothesis has to be one you could prove WRONG with a measurement. Look at your three — could each one lose? If not, rewrite it until it could.

📚 Activity 3.1.3 — Who Do You Trust?

You're researching whether a soil additive is worth buying. You find five sources. Rank them from MOST to LEAST trustworthy for making your decision, then defend your top and bottom pick in a sentence each:

  • A. The University of Arizona Cooperative Extension's published trial results on the additive.
  • B. The additive company's own website: "Farmers report up to 40% better yields!"
  • C. A peer-reviewed journal study testing the additive on three soil types.
  • D. A YouTube farmer who tried it on one garden bed for one season.
  • E. A reply in an online forum: "That stuff is snake oil, trust me."
The three questions to ask of any source: Who wrote it, and do they know the field? What's their evidence? Do they gain anything if I believe them? Write one sentence on how source B does on question three.

🦺 Activity 3.1.4 — Safety Is Part of the Process

Real agriscience happens in labs, greenhouses, barns, and shops — and every one has its hazards. Scientists don't treat safety as a rule sheet to sign; it's part of designing the work. For each setting below, write the two most important safety practices you'd insist on:

SettingYour two safety practices
Mixing a fertilizer solution in the classroom lab
Working around large animals in a pen
Using cutting tools in the shop

Ideas to work with: eye protection · gloves · read the label/instructions first · never work alone · let the animal know where you are, never directly behind it · carry tools pointed down · wash hands after · know where the eyewash/first-aid is.
