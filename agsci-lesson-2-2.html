Lesson 2.2 — Farming, Water & the Land

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Arizona grows lettuce in a desert. That's only possible because farms borrow two things from nature: water and soil. In the West, agriculture uses most of the water people take from rivers and wells — and every field of bare, plowed dirt is soil that wind and rain can carry away.

Big idea: farming changes the water and the land it depends on. Good farmers understand those effects — on water supplies, on erosion, on soil fertility — and choose methods that protect them, because a farm that ruins its own water and soil doesn't stay a farm.

Water + soil = the farm's savings account. You can spend it down, or you can farm in a way that keeps the account full.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Irrigation — bringing water to crops instead of waiting for rain (flood, sprinkler, or drip).
  • Aquifer — water stored underground in rock and gravel; wells pump it out. It refills far slower than we pump it.
  • Erosion — wind or water carrying soil away.
  • Topsoil — the dark top layer where most nutrients and life are. It takes centuries to form and one bad season to lose.
  • Soil fertility — the soil's ability to feed plants (nutrients + structure + living things).
  • Runoff — rain or irrigation water that flows off the field, carrying whatever's on it — including fertilizer and pesticides — into ditches, rivers, and lakes.
  • Algae bloom — a green explosion of algae in a lake or river, often fed by fertilizer runoff. When the algae die, they use up the oxygen fish need.
  • No-till — planting without plowing, so soil stays covered and anchored.
  • Cover crop — a crop grown to protect and feed the soil between cash crops, not to sell.

🌪 Activity 2.2.2 — The Dust Bowl: When the Soil Blew Away

Read this short account, then answer the questions below it.

In the 1930s, farmers on the Great Plains plowed up millions of acres of native grassland to plant wheat. The grass roots that had held the soil for thousands of years were gone. Then came years of drought. The bare, dry soil turned to dust, and windstorms carried it away in black clouds so thick they turned day into night — some dust traveled all the way to New York City. Thousands of farms failed, and families had to leave the land. Afterward, the government and farmers changed how they worked: planting rows of trees as windbreaks, plowing along the curves of hills instead of straight down them, and keeping soil covered instead of bare.

  1. What TWO things combined to cause the Dust Bowl? (One was human, one was weather.)
  2. What was the grass doing for the soil before it was plowed up?
  3. Name two practices that came out of the Dust Bowl to stop it from happening again.
Connect it to today: a farmer plants a cover crop after harvest instead of leaving the field bare all winter. What is that cover crop protecting the field from? One or two sentences.

📋 Project 2.2.4 — Design a Water-Wise, Soil-Wise Farm

You're starting a small vegetable farm on sloped land near a creek, in a dry climate. Design your protection plan. Choose three practices from the list (or invent your own) and for each one write what it protects (water supply, soil from erosion, or soil fertility) and how:

  • Drip irrigation · watering at night or early morning · soil moisture sensors
  • No-till planting · cover crops · plowing along the slope's curves (contour farming)
  • A strip of grass and bushes between the fields and the creek (a buffer strip)
  • Testing the soil first, and only applying the fertilizer the crop actually needs

Finish with two or three sentences: which ONE practice would you install first, and why?

Bonus (optional): Where does YOUR water come from? Ask at home or look it up — river, well, or city supply — and write one sentence about it.
