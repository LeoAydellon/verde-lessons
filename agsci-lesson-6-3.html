Lesson 6.3 — Feeding & Reproducing Plants

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

Lesson 6.1 gave you the machine; 6.2 gave you the season. This lesson is the management manual: what to FEED a crop (and how to read its complaints when something's missing), and how to make MORE plants — including the surprising fact that every Honeycrisp apple tree on Earth is the same tree, copied.

Big idea: feeding a crop is a numbers game you can win with a soil test and a little math. Reproducing a crop is a choice between two strategies — mix genes with seed, or copy a winner exactly. Good growers use both, on purpose.

The bag math that runs everything: fertilizer analysis is percent by weight. A 50-pound bag of 10-20-10 holds 10% nitrogen = 5 pounds of actual N. Every fertilizer decision starts with this one calculation.

📖 The words you'll use

  • 17 essential elements — everything a plant needs. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen come free from air and water; the other 14 come from soil or the fertilizer bag.
  • N-P-K — the three primary macronutrients on every label. Nitrogen = leafy green growth. Phosphorus = roots, flowers, fruit. Potassium = water regulation, stiff stalks, disease resistance.
  • Micronutrients — iron, zinc, boron and friends: needed in tiny amounts, but a shortage still wrecks the crop.
  • Interveinal chlorosis — yellow young leaves with green veins: the signature of iron shortage, and Arizona's most common deficiency (the soil HAS iron — pH near 8 locks it up).
  • Soilless media — greenhouse mixes: peat or coconut coir (holds water), perlite (drainage and air), vermiculite (water and nutrients).
  • Composite sample — soil cores from several spots mixed into one sample, so the test reads the field, not one lucky shovel-hole.
  • Fertigation — running nutrients through the drip line, split across the season.
  • Scarification / stratification — nicking a hard seed coat so water gets in · a moist chilling period that imitates winter.
  • Asexual propagation — clones identical to the parent: cuttings, layering, division, grafting, budding, tissue culture.
  • Scion / rootstock / cambium — in a graft: the top variety · the root system it rides on · the growing layer in both that MUST line up or the union fails.

  3. Three of the 17 essentials never appear on a fertilizer label because plants get them free. Name them and their sources.

Sticker shock: a rancher says "the 10-20-10 is cheaper per bag than the 21-0-0, so it's the better nitrogen buy." Run the math on actual N per bag (both 50 lb) and settle it. Price per POUND OF NUTRIENT is the only honest comparison.

🩺 Activity 6.3.2 — Reading the Plant's Complaints

Deficiency symptoms are a diagnostic chart — WHERE the damage shows tells you WHICH nutrient, because some nutrients can move within the plant and some can't:

  1. Nitrogen is mobile in the plant; iron is not. A nitrogen-starved corn plant yellows its OLDEST, lowest leaves first, evenly. An iron-starved peach tree yellows its YOUNGEST leaves, veins still green. Explain the logic: when a mobile nutrient runs short, where does the plant steal it from — and why can't it do that with iron?
  2. Diagnose each case (nutrient + your clue):
    CaseSymptomYour diagnosis
    1Tomatoes rotting from the blossom end while still green
    2Whole plant stunted, leaves tinged purple
    3Older leaf edges scorched brown, stalks weak
    4Newest leaves yellow, veins green, on a pH-8 Verde Valley soil
    5Lowest leaves uniformly pale yellow, plant slow all over
  3. Most crops do best between pH 6.0 and 7.0. Which direction is most Arizona soil off, which amendment moves pH DOWN, and which moves it UP?
  4. Fertilizer isn't the only fix. Name two non-bag fixes from this unit's toolbox (think Unit 4's soil lesson: what did compost and legume rotation each add?).
Yard detective: find one real plant near you showing ANY off-color symptom (yellow leaves, scorched edges, purple tinge — landscaping counts). Describe exactly WHERE on the plant the symptom sits and give your best diagnosis with the clue you used. If the plant is just thirsty, that's a legitimate diagnosis too — say why you think so.

🧪 Activity 6.3.3 — Test First, Then Feed: the Fertilizer Plan

Guessing wastes money and sends nitrogen toward the groundwater (Unit 4's runoff lesson — same villain). Professionals test, then plan:

  1. Describe how to pull a proper composite sample from a one-acre garden plot: how many spots, what tool, what happens to the cores. Why does ONE shovel-hole make a bad test?
  2. A fertilization plan answers four questions: right SOURCE, right RATE, right TIMING, right PLACEMENT. Your soil test says: pH 7.9 · nitrogen low · phosphorus adequate · potassium high. For a tomato bed, make one call for each of the four (and say which bag number you do NOT need to buy this year).
  3. Why is splitting nitrogen into several small feedings across the season — or fertigating through the drip line — better than one big dump at planting? Answer with BOTH the crop's side and the groundwater's side.
  4. A greenhouse grower starts seeds in a peat-perlite-vermiculite mix instead of field soil. Name the five jobs a good medium does, and the big hidden advantage a soilless mix has over even great field soil for starting seeds. (What did Lesson 4.3 say lives in field soil?)
The two-word rule: "Test first." A neighbor fertilizes on schedule every month, never tested, plants yellowing anyway. List the three ways that habit could be failing at once — wrong nutrient, locked pH, or already-plenty. Which of the three does a $20 soil test rule out?

🌿 Activity 6.3.4 — Start a Clone (and know when NOT to use seed)

Seed shuffles genes (Lesson 5.2's whole card game) — every seedling is a gamble. A cutting is a photocopy. Time to make one; this runs 1–3 weeks, so start now:

  • Cut a 4–6 inch piece of a soft-stemmed plant — mint, basil, pothos, coleus all root easily (grocery-store basil works; ask before cutting anyone's plant).
  • Strip the lower leaves, stand it in a jar of water on a windowsill, top up the water as needed.
  • Watch for roots from the cut end and the leaf nodes.
  1. Log it: date started, plant used, first roots seen on day ___. (Rooting hormone speeds this in a nursery — your kitchen version just takes a few days longer.)
  2. Your rooted cutting's genes: identical to the parent, or shuffled? So if the parent plant had a trait you loved, is a cutting or a seed the sure way to keep it?
  3. Seeds fight back against sprouting at the wrong time. Match the fix to the seed: a hard-coated desert mesquite seed → ______ · a peach pit that must feel winter before sprouting → ______. (scarification / stratification — and say what each fix imitates from nature.)
  4. Every Honeycrisp apple tree is a graft: a Honeycrisp scion on a hardy rootstock. Two-part question: (a) why can't orchards just plant Honeycrisp SEEDS (Lesson 5.2 knows) · (b) what layer in scion and rootstock must line up for the graft to take?
  5. Name the other asexual methods from the vocabulary you did NOT use tonight — and pick which one a lab uses to make thousands of identical disease-free plants from a few cells.
Breeder's full circle: plant breeders use SEED on purpose (they WANT the shuffle — that's where new varieties come from), then switch to CLONING the instant they find a winner. Write one sentence explaining that two-step strategy using "variation" and "identical."

Honesty rule (same as every unit): real rates depend on real soil tests. Label every number you invented as invented. A plan with named assumptions is professional; a plan with hidden ones is just hope with a spreadsheet.
