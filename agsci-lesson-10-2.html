🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

The last lesson of the year, and it points forward. Modern agriculture runs on data: a spreadsheet that recalculates your break-even the second feed prices move, a drone that spots crop stress before your eyes can, a tractor that steers itself to about an inch. None of it is magic — every tool in this lesson is a faster way of doing something this course already taught you. The drone is Lesson 8.1's scouting with wings. The yield map is Lesson 6.3's deficiency diagnosis drawn in color. The greenhouse controller is Lesson 9.1's ventilation with a brain.

Big idea: technology solves a problem only when it NARROWS the question. A tool that produces a pile of data nobody acts on is an expense, not an upgrade. The last skill of the year is deciding whether the tool earned its keep — which is Lesson 10.1's break-even thinking, aimed at the tools themselves.

📖 The words you'll use

  • GPS — a SATELLITE system that tells you precisely where you are.
  • GIS — SOFTWARE that layers located data (soil, yield, water lines) onto a map so patterns can be analyzed. Not the same thing as GPS — one locates, one analyzes.
  • UAS (drone) — unmanned aircraft system. Commercial flights require an FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate and aircraft registration.
  • Ground-truth — walking to the spot to confirm what the map claims. Every map needs it.
  • Variable rate — matching the input to what each ZONE needs instead of blanketing the whole field.
  • RTK guidance — corrected GPS that steers a tractor to about an inch.
  • LabQuest — a handheld data collector that logs probe readings (pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen) at the lab bench or the stock tank.
  • PLC — programmable logic controller: a rugged industrial computer that reads sensors and runs programmed logic (open the vents, start the fans, trip the auger).
  • CNC — computer numerical control: a digital file drives the plasma table or router so every part comes out identical.
  • AET — the electronic record book from Lesson 10.1; the software side of your SAE.

Feed costs, breeding dates, yield data — and when one number changes, everything downstream recalculates itself. Try it at home — build a GIS by hand: GIS is layered located data — you can build one with paper. Draw your yard, street, or a nearby lot to rough scale. Then make three LAYERS on separate sheets (or colors on tracing paper): (1) water — spigots, hoses, drainage, where puddles form; (2) sun — full-sun vs. shade areas at midday; (3) living things — trees, garden spots, where the dog wears the grass out. Stack them and write the analysis: name ONE pattern visible only when layers combine (e.g. "the bare patch is where full sun meets no water reach"). That pattern-from-layers moment is exactly what a $10,000 GIS package sells.

🌾 Project 10.2.4 — Fix Something With Technology (capstone)

The year ends where Lesson 3.1 began: a problem, narrowed until it can be solved. The corn field shows an unexplained yellow patch. The technology answer is a chain, not a gadget:

Fly it → map the patch → mark it with GPS → soil-sample THAT spot → treat ONLY that zone → measure the result → decide if the tool earned its keep. That's variable-rate thinking: input matched to the zone, cost cut, runoff reduced (Lesson 4.2 would approve).
  1. Walk the chain yourself for the yellow patch, one sentence per step — and name which lesson of this course each step leans on (the diagnosis step alone reaches back to 6.3's mobile-vs-immobile logic).
  2. The blanket alternative: spreading full-rate fertilizer over all 80 acres when only 6 acres needed it. Using Lesson 6.3's numbers-style: 74 unneeded acres × 150 lb/acre × $0.60/lb — what did the blanket approach waste? (Show the multiplication: 74 × 150 × 0.60.) Where does the excess go — Lesson 4.2 answered this.
  3. Now YOUR capstone: pick one real problem anywhere in your world — a struggling houseplant, a gate that's always left open, a chore everyone forgets, an animal losing weight. Write the six-step chain for it: narrow the question → choose ONE tool from this lesson (spreadsheet, phone GPS, a data log, an if-then controller, a camera) → gather the data → act on ONLY what the data shows → measure the result → verdict: did the tool earn its keep? A "the tool wasn't worth it" verdict with honest evidence earns full credit.
