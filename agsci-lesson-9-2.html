Lesson 9.2 — Tools, Equipment & Safe Operation

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

Lesson 9.1 planned the structure; this lesson is about the hands and machines that build and maintain it — and the one rule that stands over all of them: the manufacturer's manual is the standard. Not a suggestion, not "for beginners" — it's the document industry actually works by, the same way Lesson 8.1 taught you the pesticide label is the law. Under that rule live the small engines, the wood tools, four different ways of joining metal, and the electrical and hydraulic systems that run modern equipment. Every one of them is safe in the hands of someone who understands it and dangerous in the hands of someone who's guessing.

Big idea: safety in a shop isn't a poster on the wall — it's specific knowledge. Knowing WHY you pull the spark plug wire, why sharp is safe, and why you never hunt a hydraulic leak with your hand is what separates an operator from a bystander with a tool.

📖 The words you'll use

  • ANSI Z87.1 — the marking on real safety glasses. No mark, not eye protection.
  • Four-stroke engine — intake, compression, power, exhaust over TWO crankshaft revolutions; carries its oil in a crankcase sump.
  • Two-stroke engine — no sump; oil is MIXED into the gasoline (a ratio like 50:1) to lubricate the crank and cylinder.
  • Feeler gauge — the thin blades that gap a spark plug or set valve clearance.
  • Kerf — the width of wood the saw blade itself eats. Measure twice, and remember the cut isn't free.
  • SMAW (stick) — welding with a flux-coated electrode; the flux makes the shielding gas and leaves slag to chip off.
  • GMAW (MIG) — continuous wire fed with shielding gas from a cylinder; fast, no slag.
  • GTAW (TIG) — a NON-consumable tungsten electrode under argon, filler fed by hand; slow but precise on aluminum and stainless.
  • Oxy-fuel — welds, brazes, and cuts with burning gas; acetylene never above 15 psi; neutral flame for most steel; cutting works on FERROUS metal only.
  • Plasma arc cutter — cuts any electrically conductive metal, including the aluminum and stainless oxy-fuel can't.
  • Ohm's law — E = I × R: volts equal amps times ohms.
  • Pascal's law — pressure in a confined fluid transmits equally in all directions: the reason hydraulics multiply force.
  • Multimeter — reads voltage, continuity, and resistance; a rested 12-volt battery should read about 12.6 volts.

🔧 Activity 9.2.1 — Small Engines: Respect the Machine

The mower, the tiller, the blower, the pump on the stock tank — small engines run the place. Two families:

Four-strokeTwo-stroke
CycleIntake → compression → power → exhaust (2 crank revolutions) Fires every revolution
OilIn a crankcase sump — check the dipstick NO sump — oil mixed into the gas (e.g. 50:1)
Typical homeMowers, tillers, generatorsChainsaws, trimmers, blowers
  1. The single most important sentence in this lesson: what do you disconnect BEFORE reaching under a mower deck, and what does that physically make impossible? Why isn't "the switch is off" good enough?
  2. Name the four strokes in order, and say what each one is doing to the fuel-air mix in plain words (breathe in · squeeze · bang · breathe out is a fine start — make it yours).
  3. A neighbor pours straight gas into a chainsaw. What happens inside, and why? Use the word "sump." Then the mix math: 50:1 means how many ounces of oil in one gallon of gas? (A gallon is 128 oz — show the division.)
  4. The service manual schedules maintenance by HOURS of run time, not by looks. List three scheduled items from this lesson, and explain why "it still looks clean" is not a maintenance plan. (Lesson 9.1 said the same thing about structures — maintenance is scheduled, not accidental.)
  5. PPE inventory for one hour of chainsaw work: name three required items from this lesson, and what "ANSI Z87.1" printed on the glasses actually tells you that a gas-station pair doesn't.

🪚 Activity 9.2.2 — Hand Tools, Power Tools & Wood

The counterintuitive rule first: sharp is safe. A dull blade makes you PUSH, and pushing is how hands end up in the cut. The rest of tool care is just as unglamorous — clean tools, a wipe of light oil on steel, grind the mushroomed head off a cold chisel, replace cracked handles, check cords for an intact ground pin.

  1. Explain "sharp is safe" in your own words — what does the extra force behind a dull blade do the moment the blade breaks free?
  2. Tool triage: for each, fix or condemn, and why — a cold chisel with a mushroomed head · a hammer with a hairline-cracked handle · a drill cord with the ground pin snapped off · a rusty but sound hand saw. (One of these connects straight to Activity 9.2.4's green wire.)
  3. Wood vocabulary in action: you're cutting a 2×4 to exactly 46 inches. What is the kerf, which side of your pencil line does the blade ride, and what happens to your board if you cut ON the line?
  4. Crosscut vs. rip: which goes ACROSS the grain? And why does a screw near the end of a board get a pilot hole first — what is the screw doing to the wood fibers without one?
  5. Finishing sequence: sand WITH the grain, coarse grit to fine, then sealer, then topcoat. What do cross-grain scratches do under a finish, and why does grit order matter — what is each finer grit actually removing?
Try it at home — tool safety audit: find five real tools where you live (any mix — kitchen knife, screwdriver, extension cord, rake, drill). For each: name it, describe its condition honestly (edge, handle, cord, rust), pass/fail it by this lesson's standards, and write the one fix that would restore a failed tool. If a cord is in the mix, check the ground pin and say what you found. Five tools, five verdicts — that's a real shop inspection.

⚡ Activity 9.2.3 — Joining & Cutting Metal

Four ways to join or cut metal, and choosing between them IS the skill:

ProcessHow it worksBest at
SMAW (stick)Flux-coated electrode; flux makes the shielding gas + slag Outdoor repair, wind, dirty steel
GMAW (MIG)Continuous wire + cylinder shielding gas; no slag Fast, clean production work
GTAW (TIG)Non-consumable tungsten under argon; filler fed by hand Slow, precise: aluminum, stainless
Oxy-fuelBurning oxygen + acetylene; welds, brazes, cuts Cutting/heating ferrous metal in the field
Decode E7018: 70 = 70,000 psi tensile strength · 1 = all positions · 8 = low-hydrogen coating — and low-hydrogen rod must be stored DRY.
  1. Why does stick welding work fine in a windy corral while MIG struggles? Say where each process's shielding gas comes from — and what shielding gas is protecting the weld FROM.
  2. Decode E6011 using the pattern above: tensile strength? positions? Then answer: why does a low-hydrogen rod like E7018 live in a heated rod oven, and what happens to a weld made with a rod that's been sitting open in monsoon-season air?
  3. The trailer hitch and the aluminum gate: which process for each, and why? (One answer is about strength and slag; the other is about which processes CAN touch aluminum at all.)
  4. Two hard limits, one sentence each: why does acetylene stay at or below 15 psi, and why can an oxy-fuel torch CUT a steel plate but only make a mess of an aluminum one? (The cutting jet is literally BURNING the iron — so what's missing in aluminum?) What tool cuts the aluminum instead?
  5. Soldering and brazing join metal WITHOUT melting the base metal. Name one place that's exactly what you want (think thin copper water lines from Lesson 9.1's plumbing, or electronics), and why a full-melt weld would be wrong there.

🔋 Activity 9.2.4 — Electrical & Hydraulic Systems

The invisible systems. A multimeter and two laws cover most of it:

Ohm's law: E = I × R (volts = amps × ohms)
Pascal's law: pressure in a confined fluid transmits equally in all directions — small pump, big piston, big force
Rested 12-V battery: about 12.6 volts
  1. Ohm's law both ways: a circuit pushes 2 amps through 6 ohms — what voltage? A 12-volt circuit across 4 ohms — how many amps? Show E = I × R rearranged for the second one.
  2. Your tractor battery reads 11.8 volts after sitting overnight. Healthy or not? What number were you hoping for, and what are your first two checks before buying a new battery? (Work the simple things first — this lesson's troubleshooting rule.)
  3. A fuse blows. Your cousin says "just put a 30 in, the 15s keep blowing." Walk through why the fuse blew, what the right response is, and what the 30-amp fuse turns the wiring into. (Lesson 9.1's wire-gauge knowledge belongs in this answer.)
  4. Explain the log-splitter using Pascal's law: modest pump pressure, large piston, huge force — where does the multiplication come from? And why does a hydraulic system feel solid while an air (pneumatic) system feels springy? One property of the fluid answers both.
  5. The cardboard rule: why does a pinhole hydraulic leak get hunted with a piece of cardboard and NEVER a hand? What is the fluid doing at that pressure, and why is this injury famously worse than it first looks?
  6. The equipment won't lift. Write a five-step troubleshooting sequence that starts with the SIMPLE things (fluid level, filter, loose connection...) and ends at the manufacturer's chart — and say why the transmission case gets opened LAST. (This is Lesson 3.1's scientific process holding a wrench: one variable at a time.)

📕 Project 9.2.5 — The Manual Is the Standard (at home)

Pick ONE real machine where you live — mower, trimmer, blower, generator, even a car. Find its manual (the paper one, or search the model number online — every manufacturer posts PDFs). Then build a one-page operator's card for it:

  1. Identity: make, model, engine type — four-stroke or two-stroke, and ONE piece of evidence from the manual or the machine itself (dipstick? mix-ratio sticker on the tank?) proving which.
  2. Before every use: three checks the manual actually lists (not guesses — cite the page or section).
  3. Scheduled service: two maintenance items WITH their hour interval or season, straight from the manual's service table.
  4. The de-energize step: what this machine's version of "pull the spark plug wire" is — the specific action that makes accidental starting impossible before you reach into it.
  5. PPE line: what the manual requires the operator to wear.
  6. Storage: what the manual says about fuel for long storage (stabilizer? drain? run dry?).
No engine at home? Do the same card for any manualed device — a power drill, a pressure cooker, even the washing machine. The skill being graded is the same one industry pays for: finding the manufacturer's answer instead of guessing. Attach or cite the manual you used.
