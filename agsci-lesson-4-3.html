Lesson 4.3 — Soil: The Living Skin of the Farm

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

In Lesson 2.2 you watched the Dust Bowl blow half of Oklahoma into the sky. This lesson is about what that dust actually WAS — because soil isn't dirt. It's a living layer, centuries in the making, and a teaspoon of it holds billions of living things.

Big idea: soil takes hundreds of years to build and one bad season to lose. A farmer who understands what soil is made of, what lives in it, and how it leaves can keep it — and a farmer who doesn't, can't.

Half solid, half space. A good farm soil is roughly 45% mineral · 5% organic matter · 25% water · 25% air. The empty half is where roots live.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Parent material — the rock soil started as, broken down over centuries by water, ice, wind, and roots (weathering).
  • Texture — the MIX of particle sizes: sand (biggest), silt, clay (smallest). Loam is a balance of all three — drains well AND holds water, which is why growers want it.
  • Structure — a different thing from texture: how particles CLUMP into crumbs called peds. Granular crumbs let water and roots through; compacted layers block them.
  • Soil horizons — the layers in a dug hole: O (surface litter) · A (topsoil, darkest and richest) · B (subsoil where clay and minerals collect) · C (weathered parent material).
  • Caliche — a cemented calcium-carbonate hardpan common around the Verde Valley. Stops roots and drainage cold.
  • Humus — dark, stable organic material made when microbes decompose residue. Stores nutrients and water.
  • Nitrogen fixation — Rhizobium bacteria living in nodules on legume roots (like alfalfa) pull nitrogen gas from the air and turn it into a form plants can use.
  • Sheet → rill → gully — water erosion's three stages: a thin peeled layer → small channels tillage can still smooth → channels too big to farm across.
  • Land Capability Classes — land rated I–VIII: Classes I–IV can be cultivated (with increasing care), V–VIII belong in pasture, range, woodland, or wildlife.
  • Leaching fraction — extra irrigation water applied on purpose to flush salt below the root zone in arid ground.

🫙 Activity 4.3.1 — What's in a Handful of Dirt

First, the jar test — a real soil-lab tool you can run at home:

  • Fill a clear jar ⅓ with soil (not potting mix — real ground), add water to ¾, a drop of dish soap, lid on, shake hard for a minute, then let it sit overnight.
  • It settles in order of particle size: sand on the bottom (heaviest, falls first), silt in the middle, clay on top (finest, settles last). Floating bits are organic matter.
  1. Sketch your jar (or a predicted jar if you can't run it yet) and label the three layers.
  2. Estimate: which layer is thickest? So is your soil sandy, silty, clayey — or a loam?
  3. Texture vs structure, in your own words: one describes the ______ of particles, the other describes how they ______.
  4. Why would a grower rather have loam than pure sand OR pure clay? (What does sand do too fast, and clay do too slowly?)
  5. Name the five factors that decide what soil forms in a place. Which one can't a farmer do anything about, no matter how good they are?
Verde Valley special: dig here and you may hit caliche — a natural concrete layer. A farmer plants an orchard tree in a caliche-bottomed hole and the tree drowns after irrigation. Explain why, and what augering through the caliche fixes.

🦠 Activity 4.3.2 — The Living Soil

A teaspoon of healthy soil holds billions of bacteria and fungi. They're not contamination — they're the workforce.

  1. Match the worker to the job: microbes decomposing residue · Rhizobium in legume nodules · mycorrhizal fungi on roots → makes humus · extends the root's reach for water and phosphorus · turns air nitrogen into plant food.
  2. Nitrogen fertilizer costs real money. Explain why a farmer plants alfalfa or another legume in rotation, then grows corn on that field the next year.
  3. Microbes work fastest in soil that is warm, moist, and aerated. Name two soil conditions that shut the workforce down.
  4. In the hole you'd dig for the project below, which horizon should be darkest — and WHY is it darkest? (Think about where residue and humus enter the soil.)
Think it through: Arizona soils are hot and low in organic matter, so they cycle nutrients differently than Midwest soils. If humus stores water and nutrients, what's ONE management move an Arizona grower can make to build it?

🌬 Activity 4.3.3 — Keep the Soil Home

Every erosion control ever invented does one of three jobs: slow the water · cover the soil · break the wind.

  1. Put water erosion's three stages in order and say which stage is the last one a farmer can still fix with tillage.
  2. Sort each practice into its job (some do two): contour farming (tilling across the slope) · windbreak trees along the field edge · cover crops · no-till with residue left on the surface · terraces · grassed waterways · strip cropping.

⛰ Activity 4.3.4 — Read the Land

Steeper slope → faster runoff → less soaking in → more soil moving. That's why land gets rated in Capability Classes I–VIII before anyone decides what to grow on it.

  1. Assign each parcel a sensible class range and a use: (a) a nearly level river-bottom field with deep loam · (b) a gently rolling field that needs contour farming · (c) an 18% rocky slope · (d) a steep canyon side. (Uses: row crops · cultivated with care · permanent grass or hay · wildlife and woodland.)
  2. What's the dividing line between Classes I–IV and V–VIII — what can you DO on the first group that you shouldn't on the second?
  3. Arid-ground problem: irrigation water evaporates, salt stays behind, and the field slowly poisons itself. What is the leaching fraction, and why does it only work if the field also has drainage?
  4. Why does every good management plan start with a soil test instead of a fertilizer purchase?
The matching rule: "Match the practice to the site; no single plan fits every field." Explain in one sentence why the same alfalfa plan can't run on parcels (a) and (c).

📋 Project 4.3.5 — Survey Your Own Ground

Be the soil surveyor for one real spot — a yard, a garden bed, a corner of the school grounds, a field edge (get permission before digging anywhere that isn't yours):

  • Dig a small hole about as deep as a shovel head. Sketch the wall of the hole: layers, colors, roots, anything cemented. Label your best guess at horizons (O? A? B?). Photograph it if you can.
  • Jar test the soil from the top layer (Activity 4.3.1). Report the texture verdict.
  • Rate it: slope (flat, gentle, steep?), bare or covered, wind exposure. Give it your own erosion-risk grade (low / medium / high) with one sentence of evidence per factor.
  • Prescribe: two management practices from this lesson that fit YOUR spot, and one practice that would be a waste of money here — and why.
  • Fill the hole back in. Surveyors leave the site the way they found it.
Honesty rule (same as Unit 3): report what your hole and jar actually showed — even if it's "two inches of gravel over hard caliche." A true boring result beats an invented interesting one, in science and in farming.
