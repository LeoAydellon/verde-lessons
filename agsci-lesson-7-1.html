Unit 7 · Agriscience III

Lesson 7.1 — Animal Science Principles

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🐄 What this lesson is about

You've spent two units on plants. Now we cross the fence. Animal agriculture runs on the exact same science you already have — cells (Unit 5), genetics (Lesson 5.2), even binomial names (Lesson 6.1) — but it starts with something plants never needed: precise language. A rancher who says "I'm selling a steer" and one who says "I'm selling a bull" are describing two very different animals at two very different prices. Get the words wrong at a sale barn and it costs real money.

Big idea: animal science begins with naming things exactly — what the animal IS (species and classification), what sex and age it is (the terminology grid), and what's been done to it (the common production practices). This lesson gives you the whole vocabulary, plus the classification systems that organize every animal from a 1,400-pound bull to the dog on your porch.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Livestock — animals raised on a farm or ranch to produce something: meat, milk, eggs, fiber, or work.
  • Large stock — the big animals: cattle and horses (many ranchers include bison and elk here too).
  • Small stock — sheep, goats, swine (pigs), and poultry (chickens, turkeys, ducks).
  • Companion animal — an animal kept for the relationship, not for production: dogs, cats, most horses kept for riding, a classroom rabbit.
  • Castration — removing the testicles of a male not chosen for breeding. Castrated males are calmer, safer to handle, and their meat grades better.
  • Dehorning / polled — removing horns young, vs. an animal BORN hornless because it carries the polled gene. (Lesson 5.2 flashback: polled is the dominant allele — that Punnett-square bull problem was real ranch math.)
  • Tail docking — shortening the tail (common in sheep to keep manure from collecting and causing flystrike).
  • Identification — how you know WHICH animal is which: ear tags, tattoos, brands, microchips, and electronic RFID tags that a reader can scan.
  • Taxonomy — the kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species filing system for every living thing. Same system you used for Medicago sativa.
  • Alternative livestock — species outside the usual list raised for niche markets: bison, elk, deer, antelope, alpacas, ostrich.

  2. All the mammals on that table share kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, and class Mammalia. The chicken splits off at class. What class is a chicken in, and name ONE body feature that puts it there instead of Mammalia.
  3. Bos taurus and Bos indicus share a genus. Based on how the system worked for plants, what does sharing a genus tell you about how closely related they are — and why would an Arizona rancher in hot country care that Bos indicus blood exists?

Pattern check: taxonomy files animals by ANCESTRY. But a rancher ALSO files animals by USE (dairy vs. beef vs. companion). One cow can be "Mammalia" and "dairy" at the same time. Write one sentence explaining why agriculture needs both filing systems.

🐕 Activity 7.1.3 — Large Stock, Small Stock, Companion

The use-based sort seems easy — until you hit the edge cases, and agriculture is FULL of edge cases:

  1. Sort into large stock, small stock, or companion: beef steer · laying hen · dairy goat · quarter horse used for ranch work · border collie that herds sheep · pet rabbit · sow · barn cat.
  2. At least two animals on that list could honestly go in more than one column. Pick one, put it in BOTH columns, and defend each placement in a sentence.
  3. The border collie and the barn cat never get sold for meat, milk, or fiber — yet many ranchers would call them working animals, not pets. What are they "producing" that earns their keep?
  4. A 4-H kid raises a lamb for the county fair, halter-breaks it, names it, and shows it — then it sells at the market auction. Companion or small stock? There's no single right answer: give YOUR answer and the reasoning, in two or three sentences. (This tension is real for every kid who's done it, and Lesson 7.2's welfare section picks the thread back up.)

🦬 Activity 7.1.4 — Beyond Cattle: Alternative Livestock

Not every ranch runs cows. Bison, elk, deer, antelope, alpacas, and ostrich are all raised commercially in the U.S. — sometimes because the animal fits land that cattle handle poorly, sometimes because a niche market pays premium prices (bison burgers, alpaca fleece, ostrich leather).

  1. Pick ONE alternative species from the list and find out: what product(s) it's raised for, one way raising it differs from raising cattle (fencing? handling? temperament? feed?), and roughly what its product sells for compared to the ordinary version. Cite where you looked.
  2. Alternative livestock usually sell into small niche markets. Explain one advantage and one danger of betting your ranch on a niche market instead of the huge ordinary beef market. (Lesson 1.2's supply-and-demand thinking applies directly.)
  3. Bison and elk are native to North America; cattle are not. Name one way an animal that EVOLVED on this continent's dry rangeland might fit that land better than an imported species. (Unit 4's rangeland logic is the hint.)
Reality check: alpacas went through a famous speculative bubble — for a while breeding stock sold for tens of thousands of dollars, far above what their fleece could ever earn back, and then prices collapsed. One sentence: what were buyers actually paying for during the bubble, if it wasn't the fleece?

📋 Project 7.1.5 — The Species File

Start a one-page "species file" on ONE production animal you can realistically learn about — one you own, a neighbor's, or one you pick from the grid. You'll add to this animal in Lessons 7.2 and 7.3, so choose one you're willing to stick with:

  • Name it precisely: species, binomial name (correct capitalization), and the full terminology row for it (male / female / castrated male / young female / baby).
  • Classify it twice: its taxonomy down to species, AND its use category (large stock, small stock, or companion — defend your call if it's an edge case).
  • Production practices: list two practices from this lesson (castration, dehorning, tail docking, identification) that are commonly done for your species, and in one sentence each, the practical reason why.
  • One number: find roughly what one animal of your species sells for (or costs to buy) right now, and where you found it.
Honesty rule (same as every unit): if a fact wouldn't pin down — prices vary, or sources disagreed — say so and give the range you found. A labeled range beats a fake exact number.
