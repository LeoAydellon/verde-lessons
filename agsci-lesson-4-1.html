Lesson 4.1 — Energy from Agriculture

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

A farm is really an energy machine. Sunlight goes in, food comes out — but that's not the only trick. Farms can also MAKE power: manure can run a generator, corn can become fuel, and the open land between crops is exactly where wind turbines and solar panels want to be.

Big idea: anything that holds stored carbon — manure, corn starch, used fryer oil, wood waste — can become energy. And because farms have the one thing energy projects need most (open land), agriculture and renewable energy fit together naturally.

Sunlight → plants → food, fuel, and power. A farm captures energy; a smart farm captures it more than once.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Renewable energy — energy from sources that don't run out: sun, wind, and things we can grow again.
  • Biofuel — fuel made from living (or recently living) material. Ethanol comes from fermented corn starch; biodiesel from soybean or used cooking oil; biomass is crop residue or wood waste burned for heat and power.
  • Anaerobic digester — a sealed tank where bacteria break down manure WITHOUT oxygen, releasing biogas (mostly methane) that can run a generator.
  • Intermittency — the catch with sun and wind: no sun at night, no power in dead calm.
  • Agrivoltaics — growing crops in the partial shade UNDER solar panels. Tested at the University of Arizona; the shade can cut heat stress and water demand.
  • Food chain — energy passing from producers (plants) to herbivores to predators, with decomposers returning nutrients to the soil.
  • The 10% rule — only about 10% of the energy at one level of a food chain makes it to the next level.
  • Sustainable — a practice that holds up three ways at once: environmentally sound, economically viable, and socially workable.

💩 Activity 4.1.1 — The Manure Power Plant

A dairy has a problem that never takes a day off: manure. An anaerobic digester turns that problem into three products. Trace the path and answer:

  1. Put these steps in order: generator makes electricity · bacteria digest manure with no oxygen · manure is collected into the sealed tank · biogas (methane) is captured.
  2. The gas is captured and burned — but the leftover SOLIDS aren't waste either. Name the two farm uses they still have.
  3. Methane from an open manure pile just drifts into the air. Give two reasons capturing it in a digester is better than letting that happen.
  4. Why does a digester make more sense for a 2,000-cow dairy than for a farm with 5 cows? (Think about what the "fuel supply" is.)
Think it through: a digester turns a disposal COST into electricity, bedding, and fertilizer. Which of the three legs of sustainability does that help — environmental, economic, or both? Explain in one sentence.

🔗 Activity 4.1.3 — Follow the Energy

Zoom out and the whole farm is a food chain: plants capture sunlight, animals eat plants, decomposers return nutrients to the soil. But energy leaks at every step — only about 10% passes from one level to the next.

  1. Draw a food chain with four levels: alfalfa → grasshopper → lizard → hawk. Label each level (producer, herbivore, predator…). Where do decomposers fit?
  2. If the alfalfa in a field captures 100,000 units of energy, about how much reaches the grasshoppers? The lizards? The hawk? (Apply the 10% rule at each step.)
  3. Use the 10% rule to explain why an acre of grain can feed more people eaten directly as bread than fed to cattle first.
  4. Now the flip side: rangeland grass is something humans cannot digest at all. Why is grazing cattle on land too dry or rocky to grow crops actually a SMART use of that land?
Careful: questions 3 and 4 are not a contradiction — they're the same rule applied to two different kinds of land. Write one sentence that says when feeding plants to animals wastes energy, and when it captures energy we couldn't get any other way.

🏷 Activity 4.1.4 — Read the Label

Three words show up on food labels and in arguments — and they don't mean what most shoppers think:

  • USDA Certified Organic — no synthetic pesticides or fertilizers, no GMO seed, no routine antibiotics or added hormones, and the ground itself must be clean of banned inputs for three years before certification. Costs more to produce, yields less per acre, sells at a premium.
  • Conventional — uses synthetic inputs and biotechnology to get the most yield at the lowest cost per unit.
  • "Natural" (on a meat label) — only means minimally processed with no artificial ingredients. It says NOTHING about how the animal was raised.
  1. A package of chicken says "All Natural." A shopper assumes the chickens lived outdoors with no antibiotics. What does the label actually guarantee?
  2. Why does organic food cost more at the store? Give two production reasons from the definition.
  3. A farmer wants to switch a field to certified organic. Why can't they sell organic crops from it next season?
  4. Is "organic" automatically the same thing as "sustainable"? Use the three-legged stool (environment · economics · people) to answer: what would it take for an organic farm — or a conventional one — to truly count as sustainable?
The stool test: "A practice that loses money every year is not sustainable — no matter how green it looks." Explain why that sentence is true, in your own words.

📋 Project 4.1.5 — Energy Plan for a Verde Valley Farm

You're the consultant. The farm: 300 acres of irrigated crops, a 500-cow dairy, strong afternoon sun, steady spring winds, and a remote stock tank with no power line. Write a one-page energy plan:

  • Recommend two energy projects from this lesson (digester, wind lease, solar pump, agrivoltaics, biofuel from crop residue…). For each: what it produces, what it costs the farm in land or money, and what it earns or saves.
  • Name one project you considered and REJECTED for this farm, and why.
  • Finish with the stool test: show your plan passes all three legs — environment, economics, people.
Make it real: a plan that says "install everything" isn't a plan. Consultants get hired for what they'd do FIRST and what they'd skip.
