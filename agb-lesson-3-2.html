Lesson 3.2 — Employment Opportunity

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Starting your own business isn't free — even if you don't spend a dime, you give up the money you could have earned at a regular job. This lesson is about weighing that trade: what your skills and time are worth, what it costs to live, and how to figure out whether running your own business actually pays off compared to working for someone else.

Big idea: the real cost of your business includes the paycheck you gave up to run it. That's called opportunity cost.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Resume — a one-page list of your skills, experience, and education, used to show you're a good fit for a job or opportunity.
  • Opportunity cost — what you give up by choosing one option over another (the wages you skip to run your own business).
  • Living expenses — what it costs you to live for a period (rent, food, gas, phone).
  • Living wage — the income you need to cover your living expenses.
  • Return to labor and management (RL&M) — what's left for you as the owner after the business pays all its other costs. It's your reward for your work and decisions.
  • Chronological — in time order (most resumes list your most recent experience first).

📝 Project 3.2.1 — Your Skills Resume

Make a simple resume for yourself. Include:

  • Name and how to reach you.
  • Skills — at least 4 things you can do (work with animals, use tools, customer service, computer skills…).
  • Experience — any jobs, chores, projects, or volunteering, listed most recent first.
  • Education — your school and any certifications.
Answer: Pick one skill on your resume and explain how it would help you run the business you've been planning.

⏰ Activity 3.2.2 — The Value of Your Time

Your time has a dollar value — it's what someone would pay you for it.

Value of your time = Hours worked × Pay rate
  1. You could work 20 hours a week at a job paying $14/hour. What's that per week?
  2. What's it over a month (about 4 weeks)?
  3. If you instead spend those 20 hours a week on your own business, that monthly amount is your opportunity cost — the money you gave up. Write it down.
Answer: Why should you count the wages you gave up as a real cost of starting your business?

🏠 Activity 3.2.3 — Income vs. Living Expenses

A job (or a business) only works if it covers what it costs you to live. Here's a monthly budget:

  1. Add up the total living expenses per month.
  2. Job A pays $1,100/month. Does it cover your living expenses? By how much over or under?

💼 Activity 3.2.4 — Return to Labor & Management

RL&M is what's left over for you after the business pays everything else. It's your reward for doing the work and making the decisions.

RL&M = Total income − All business expenses (not counting your own pay)

Work it out: A roadside stand brought in $18,000 this year. Its business expenses (supplies, fuel, insurance, interest) added up to $11,000.

  1. What is the RL&M — the amount left for you?
  2. You spent about 500 hours running it this year. Divide the RL&M by 500 — what did you effectively earn per hour?
  3. Is that better or worse than the $14/hour job from Activity 3.2.2?
Answer: Based on the numbers, would you keep running the stand or take the job? Explain your choice.

🏦 Problem 3.2.5 — Would a Lender Say Yes?

When you ask a lender for a loan, they check whether your business is strong enough to pay it back. They look at your income, your expenses, and whether there's money left over after everything (including a loan payment).

Your business: income $18,000/year, expenses $11,000/year. You want a loan whose payments would be $4,000/year.

  1. After expenses, how much does the business have left before the loan payment?
  2. Would that leftover cover the $4,000 loan payment? By how much?
  3. Do you think a lender would approve this loan? Why?
Answer: Why does a lender care about your leftover income instead of just your total sales?
