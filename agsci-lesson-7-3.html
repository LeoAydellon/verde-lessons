Lesson 7.3 — Managing Animals: Nutrition, Breeding & Health

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🧑‍🌾 What this lesson is about

Lesson 7.1 gave you the words. Lesson 7.2 gave you the animal. This lesson is the JOB: the four decisions a producer makes over and over — what to feed, which animals to breed, how to keep them healthy, and what the products are finally worth. Every one of them is applied science you already own: the feed tag is Lesson 5.2's macromolecules, the breeding decisions are Lesson 5.2's Punnett squares grown up, and the withdrawal rules point straight at Unit 8's food safety.

Big idea: management is nutrition + genetics + health, run on a calendar, written down. The producers who thrive are the ones with records; "I think I wormed her in the spring" is how animals and money get lost.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Six nutrient classes — water, carbohydrates, protein, fats, vitamins, minerals. (Water is first on purpose: it's the nutrient that kills fastest when it's missing.)
  • Ration — the total feed an animal gets in a day. A balanced ration supplies every nutrient that animal's life stage needs.
  • Crude protein (CP) — the protein percentage on every feed tag; the number rations are usually balanced around.
  • Maintenance vs. production — a dry cow standing around needs maintenance only; a lactating cow, a growing steer, a hen laying daily, or a working horse needs maintenance PLUS production nutrients.
  • By-products — everything the animal yields besides the obvious cut of meat: leather, tallow, gelatin, lanolin from wool, bone meal. The saying at the packing plant: "everything but the moo."
  • Wholesale cuts — the big sections a carcass is first split into (beef: chuck, rib, loin, round, brisket, plate, flank).
  • Selection — choosing WHICH animals get to be parents. Breeding — the planned mating itself. Genetics — the science that predicts what the offspring will inherit. Reproduction — the biology that turns the plan into a live baby.
  • EPD (Expected Progeny Difference) — a number published for breeding bulls predicting how their calves will compare to another bull's (birth weight, growth, milk). Selection by data instead of by eyeball.
  • Hybrid vigor (heterosis) — crossbred offspring outperforming the average of their purebred parents. The reason so many commercial herds are crosses.
  • Breeding soundness — a pre-season exam proving an animal CAN breed: body condition, structure (feet and legs), reproductive exam.
  • Gestation — pregnancy length. Withdrawal time — the required wait between an animal's last dose of a medication and its milk or meat entering the food supply.

➗ Activity 7.3.2 — Balancing a Ration: the Pearson Square

Here's the classic tool for mixing two feeds to hit a target protein. It's just subtraction arranged in a square. Say a growing pig needs a 16% CP ration, and you have corn (9% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP):

Put 16 in the middle. Corn 9 and soybean meal 44 on the left corners.
Subtract diagonally, ignore the signs:
44 − 16 = 28 parts corn  ·  16 − 9 = 7 parts soybean meal
Total 35 parts → corn = 28/35 = 80% of the mix, soybean meal = 7/35 = 20%.
Check: (0.80 × 9) + (0.20 × 44) = 7.2 + 8.8 = 16% CP ✓
  1. Re-do the worked example yourself, showing the check line. (Yes, actually write it — the check line is where mistakes surface.)
  2. Your turn: a ewe flock needs a 12% CP mix from the same corn (9%) and soybean meal (44%). Run the square, give both percentages, and CHECK it.
  3. For a 500-pound batch of your question-2 mix: how many pounds of corn, how many of soybean meal?
  4. Soybean meal costs more per pound than corn. Using your two answers, one sentence: why does a producer who over-shoots protein "waste money twice"? (Hint: the extra protein isn't stored — the urinary system from Lesson 7.2 deals with it.)
  5. Reality guardrail: the square only balances ONE nutrient. Name two other things from Activity 7.3.1 a real ration still has to get right.

🥩 Activity 7.3.3 — What the Animal Is Worth: Products & Cuts

The carcass isn't sold as "beef" — it's split into wholesale cuts, and they are NOT equal. The tender, expensive cuts (rib, loin) come from muscles along the back that did little work; the tougher, cheaper, bigger cuts (chuck, round) come from the shoulder and hind leg that carried the animal its whole life. That's Lesson 7.2's musculoskeletal system showing up at the meat counter.

  1. Match each species to its famous wholesale/retail cuts: beef — chuck, rib, loin, round · pork — shoulder, loin, ham, belly (bacon!) · poultry — breast, wing, thigh, drumstick. Now answer: which beef cut and which pork cut come from the SAME region of the animal (use your Lesson 7.2 landmark map)?
  2. Explain, muscle-by-muscle, why ribeye costs several times more per pound than chuck roast — and why slow-cooking (a stew, a smoker) can make the cheap cut delicious anyway.
  3. By-products: list five things in your house that likely contain something from a processed animal besides meat. (Candidates to investigate: leather shoes, gelatin in marshmallows or gummy candy, soap/tallow, lanolin hand cream, bone meal in garden fertilizer — that last one closes a loop with Lesson 6.3.)
  4. Fiber check: which animals from this unit are raised partly or mostly for FIBER, and name the fiber. (Two ordinary, one alternative from Lesson 7.1.)
Meat-counter field trip: next grocery run, find the per-pound price of one loin-region cut and one chuck/round-region cut of the same species. Write both prices down. Does the low-work-muscle rule hold at YOUR store?

🧬 Activity 7.3.4 — Choosing the Parents: Selection, Breeding & Genetics

Lesson 5.2 ended with you designing a one-trait breeding plan. Here's the professional version of that same thinking:

  1. Four words that get mixed up: selection, breeding, genetics, reproduction. Write one sentence for a cattle herd using ALL FOUR correctly. (Example shape: "Using ___ data, the rancher's ___ of a polled bull set up a ___ program that depends on healthy ___.")
  2. The polled problem, round two: your cows are horned (recessive, hh). Bull A is polled Hh; Bull B is polled HH. Punnett-square both matings (Lesson 5.2 skills). What fraction of each bull's calves will be polled? Which bull ends dehorning on your ranch in ONE generation, and why is that a welfare win (Lesson 7.2)?
  3. EPDs: Bull X has a LOWER birth-weight EPD and a HIGHER growth EPD than Bull Y. You're breeding first-calf heifers (small, first pregnancy). Which bull, and what's the calving-difficulty logic?
  4. Hybrid vigor: why do commercial producers often cross two breeds even though purebred breeders look down on "mutts"? And why must someone still maintain the purebred lines?
  5. Breeding soundness: name three things checked before a bull or ram goes out for the season, and what one un-checked infertile male costs (think: how many females did he "cover" — and how many calves arrive next spring?).

🩺 Activity 7.3.5 — Keeping Them Alive: Health, Sanitation & Withdrawal

A sick animal announces itself if you know the signs: off feed, isolated from the group, head down, rough coat, abnormal manure, temperature out of range (a cow's normal is about 101.5°F — yours is 98.6°F; comparative anatomy again). The professional response is a PLAN, not a panic:

The treatment-plan skeleton (standard 10.7): 1) Observe and write down the signs → 2) Isolate the animal (protects the herd) → 3) Diagnose — with your vet when it's beyond you → 4) Treat, exactly as labeled → 5) Record everything: date, animal ID, product, dose, and the withdrawal date.
  1. Use the skeleton: your ewe is off feed, standing apart, breathing fast on a hot afternoon. Write her five-step plan — including which step Lesson 7.1's ear tag makes possible, and what your thermometer contributes at step 1.
  2. Sanitation order matters: the rule is clean FIRST (scrub away manure and organic matter), THEN disinfect. Why does disinfectant sprayed onto a dirty surface largely fail? (Unit 5's microbe knowledge: what is the organic matter doing for the germs?)
  3. Name three pieces of equipment or places on a farm that need routine clean-then-disinfect, and one disease-spreading habit the routine prevents. (Water troughs, feed buckets, needles/syringes, trailers between loads, the lambing pen between ewes — pick from these or your own.)
  4. Withdrawal times: a medication's label says "milk withdrawal 72 hours; slaughter withdrawal 28 days." Explain in plain English what the dairy must do with that cow's milk for three days — and exactly why step 5 of the skeleton (the written record) is the only thing standing between an honest mistake and medicated milk in the tank. (This is Unit 8's food-safety door, already opening.)
  5. Prevention beats treatment: using Lesson 7.2 (vaccines train the immune system; colostrum; welfare basics; low-stress handling), list three things a producer does BEFORE animals get sick that shrink the vet bill after.

📋 Project 7.3.6 — The Management Plan (Species File, Final Page)

Finish your species file: write a one-page management plan for YOUR species, as if you were about to raise one (or five) for real:

  • Feeding plan: what it eats at maintenance vs. its production stage (growing, laying, lactating, or working) — name the feeds and roughly the CP% target for the production stage, with your source.
  • Breeding plan: gestation (or incubation) length, the season you'd time births for in the Verde Valley and why, and ONE trait you'd select the parents for — with how you'd check it (EPD, breeding-soundness exam, or a Punnett-square prediction).
  • Health calendar: at least three dated entries for a year (a vaccination, a parasite treatment with its withdrawal note, a breeding-soundness or pregnancy check) — plus your clean-then-disinfect routine, named.
  • The exit: what product finally leaves the farm, roughly what it sells for (your Lesson 7.1 number, updated if you've learned better), and one by-product buyers get from your species.
Honesty rule (same as every unit): real plans cite real numbers with sources — and where sources disagreed (protein targets vary by breed and stage), give the range and say whose number you trusted more, and why.
