Lesson 6.1 — How Plants Are Built & How They Work

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

Every crop in Arizona — the Yuma lettuce from Lesson 1.1, an alfalfa field, a backyard tomato — is running the same four-part machine: roots, stems, leaves, flowers. In Unit 5 you went INSIDE the cell and found chloroplasts and mitochondria. This lesson zooms back out and asks: how do those parts work together as one living factory?

Big idea: a plant is a factory with a water intake (roots), a two-way pipeline (stem), a solar-powered production floor (leaves), and a reproduction department (flowers). Understand the four jobs and you can diagnose almost any plant problem you'll ever see.

The two reactions that run everything:
Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water + light → glucose + oxygen (in the chloroplasts).
Respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (in the mitochondria — day AND night).

📖 The words you'll use

  • Taproot / fibrous roots — one deep main root (alfalfa, carrots) vs a shallow spreading net (corn, lawn grass). Root hairs multiply the absorbing surface enormously.
  • Xylem — the pipe that carries water and minerals UP from the roots.
  • Phloem — the pipe that carries sugar FROM the leaves to wherever it's needed. (Memory trick: phloem moves the food.)
  • Stomata — tiny pores on the leaf, opened and closed by guard cells. CO₂ comes in, water vapor goes out.
  • Stamen — the male flower part: anther (makes pollen) on a filament.
  • Pistil — the female flower part: stigma (catches pollen), style, and ovary. After fertilization the ovary becomes the FRUIT and the ovules become the SEEDS.
  • Transpiration — water evaporating out through the stomata. It pulls the whole water column up the xylem and cools the leaf, like sweating.
  • Monocot / dicot — one seed leaf, parallel veins, fibrous roots, flower parts in 3s (corn, wheat) vs two seed leaves, netted veins, taproot, parts in 4s or 5s (beans, tomatoes).
  • Annual / biennial / perennial — one season (corn, lettuce) · two years (carrots, onions) · lives for years (alfalfa, grapevines, apple trees).
  • Binomial name — every plant's two-word Latin name: genus capitalized, species lowercase. Alfalfa is Medicago sativa.

🥬 Activity 6.1.2 — The Xylem Highway (a real experiment)

You can watch the xylem work with stuff from the kitchen. This runs overnight — start it today:

  • Put about an inch of water in a cup and add enough food coloring to make it strong (red or blue shows best).
  • Stand a fresh celery stalk in it — leaves still on if possible. (No celery? A white flower, a green onion, or a napa cabbage leaf works.)
  • Wait overnight. Then look at the leaves — and cut the stalk crosswise near the bottom.
  1. Describe what you saw in the leaves after a day. Where did the color come from?
  2. In your cut cross-section: the color shows up as separate dots, not spread through the whole stalk. What ARE those dots — and why does that prove water travels in dedicated pipes instead of soaking upward like a sponge?
  3. Nothing in this experiment pumped the water. What actually PULLED it up the stalk? Name the process and where the water exits the plant.
  4. Unit 5 callback: water crossed into the celery's cells without the plant spending any energy. Which transport process from Lesson 5.1 is that — diffusion, osmosis, or active transport?
Farm connection: on a 105° June day in the Verde Valley, transpiration runs fast — every open stoma is losing water to that dry air. Explain why drip irrigation (Unit 4) fits this climate better than a sprinkler throwing water into the air above the crop.

🔬 Activity 6.1.3 — Sorting the Plant Kingdom

Growers classify plants three different ways at once — by structure, by lifespan, and by name:

  1. Monocot or dicot? Sort: corn · pinto bean · wheat · tomato · lawn grass · sunflower. For each, name ONE clue you could check in the field (veins, roots, seed leaves, or flower-part count).
  2. Annual, biennial, or perennial? Sort: lettuce · carrot · alfalfa · corn · apple tree · onion. Then answer: why does a farmer only have to plant an alfalfa stand once every several years but must replant corn every single spring?
  3. Write alfalfa's Latin name correctly (capitalization matters). Why do scientists worldwide bother with Latin names when "alfalfa" already works fine in English? (Hint: what's alfalfa called in other countries?)
  4. A seed catalog lists a plant as a "horticultural food crop" and another as an "agronomic field crop." Give one example of each from crops you've met in this course.
Field test: go outside and find one real monocot and one real dicot (a lawn and almost any weed will do). Write down which clue you used for each. If you guessed wrong on your first try, say so — noticing the wrong clue and fixing it is the skill.

☀️ Activity 6.1.4 — Energy In, Energy Out

Photosynthesis and respiration are the same equation running in opposite directions — and mixing them up is the classic exam mistake:

  1. Write both equations (words are fine). Then label each: which one STORES energy in glucose, and which one RELEASES it for the plant to use?
  2. True or false, and fix it if false: "Plants photosynthesize during the day and respire only at night." Explain what respiration is doing at 2 PM on a sunny day.
  3. Which organelle from Unit 5 runs photosynthesis, and which runs respiration?
  4. A storage shed full of baled hay is respiring (the plant cells are slowly burning their own glucose). Alfalfa baled too wet — above about 18–20% moisture — can mold and heat enough to combust. Using "respiration releases energy," explain where that barn-fire heat comes from.
  5. Transpiration is the third T in the story. Name two jobs it does for the plant — and one way it hurts the grower's water budget in a desert state.
One-breath summary: you eat glucose (well, food that contains it) and breathe oxygen for the exact same reason a corn plant runs respiration. Write one sentence connecting what your mitochondria and a corn plant's mitochondria are both doing right now.

📋 Project 6.1.5 — Plant Autopsy

Pick ONE real plant you can actually get your hands on — a weed from the yard (ask first before pulling anything from a garden), a houseplant, a vegetable with its top still on, anything living:

  • Identify the four structures you can find on it (roots, stem, leaves, flower/fruit — if a structure is missing, say why: season? harvested? juvenile plant?).
  • Classify it three ways: monocot or dicot (name your clue) · annual, biennial, or perennial (look it up) · its two-word Latin name, written with correct capitalization.
  • Trace a drop of water through it in words: from the soil, through which structures and which pipe, to where it exits — naming the process that pulls it the whole way.
  • Diagnose: name one thing this plant's structure tells you about how it should be watered or where it would grow best (root type, leaf size, stem toughness — use the evidence).
Honesty rule (same as every unit): if you can't find your plant's Latin name or lifespan from a reliable source, say so and state your best guess AS a guess. A labeled guess beats a confident wrong answer.
