Lesson 2.3 — Tough Questions, Real Evidence

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌱 What this lesson is about

A farmer doesn't just answer to the weather. There are laws about who gets river water, how workers are treated, what a food label must say, and how animals are kept. And there are arguments — about climate, about chemicals, about how land should be used — where everyone has an opinion and it's loud.

Big idea: laws shape what farms can do, and evidence — not the loudest voice — is how you decide where you stand. In this lesson you'll practice both: seeing how legislation touches agriculture, and telling real evidence apart from opinion when a topic gets controversial.

Opinions are free. Evidence is earned. A strong position is an opinion that evidence actually supports.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Legislation — laws passed by government (state or federal) that people and businesses must follow.
  • Regulation — the specific rules agencies write to carry out a law.
  • Water rights — the legal right to take water from a river or well; in the West, older rights usually get water first.
  • Tariff — a tax on goods crossing a border; changes what farm products are worth in trade.
  • Biosecurity — rules and practices that keep diseases and pests from spreading between farms.
  • Animal welfare — standards for how farm animals are housed, handled, and cared for.
  • Evidence — facts, measurements, and study results that can be checked. Not the same as a claim or a feeling.
  • Bias — a lean in how information is presented, often because the source gains something if you believe it.
  • Data — recorded measurements (rainfall, yields, prices, counts) you can analyze to solve problems.

Each law below touches a real farm somewhere. Copy the table and write in what changes on the farm because of it:

🔍 Activity 2.3.2 — Evidence or Opinion?

Label each statement E (evidence — checkable fact or measurement) or O (opinion — a judgment or feeling, even if you agree with it):

  1. "The monitoring wells show the aquifer dropped 4 feet over the last ten years."
  2. "Organic food just tastes better."
  3. "In our trial, the no-till field lost half as much soil in the rainstorm as the plowed field."
  4. "Farmers today don't care about the land like they used to."
  5. "The county's average summer temperature has risen 2°F since 1980, according to weather-station records."
  6. "Nobody should ever use pesticides for any reason."
Then answer: Take one of the OPINION statements and write what evidence would be needed to actually support it — or to knock it down. One or two sentences.

📊 Activity 2.3.3 — Let the Data Talk

A farmer tested two fields for three years. Same crop, same seed — Field A got a soil test each spring and only the fertilizer the test called for; Field B got the same fixed amount every year, no test. Here are the yields (bushels per acre) and fertilizer costs per acre:

  1. Which field cost more to fertilize, and by roughly how much per acre over the three years?
  2. Did the extra money buy a bigger harvest? Use the numbers in your answer.
  3. What would you tell this farmer to do in Year 4 — and what's your evidence?
Why this matters: this is how real farms use data — soil tests, yield monitors, price records — to make decisions instead of guessing. One sentence: name one other measurement this farmer could start recording, and what question it would help answer.

📋 Project 2.3.4 — Take a Stand (the Evidence Way)

Choose ONE controversial topic that touches agriculture:

  • Should desert cities or desert farms get first claim on limited river water?
  • How should farming respond to a changing climate?
  • Should there be stricter limits on pesticides?
  • Is it worth paying farmers to protect soil (cover crops, no-till) with public money?

Write three short paragraphs: (1) the strongest case for one side, using at least one piece of evidence from this unit or your own research; (2) the strongest case for the other side, same rule; (3) where YOU land — and the single piece of evidence that moved you most.

The rule: every claim gets an "according to…" — a source, a measurement, or a study. A paragraph with no evidence is just louder opinion.
