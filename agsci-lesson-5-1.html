Lesson 5.1 — Cells, DNA & the Code of Life

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🔬 What this lesson is about

In Unit 4 you learned that a teaspoon of soil holds billions of living things. This unit zooms all the way in — to the cell, the smallest piece of anything that's alive. Every farming decision you'll ever make lands on cells: water in or out of them, nutrients across their membranes, energy burned inside them.

Big idea: farming is applied biology. A wilted plant, an over-fertilized field that burned, a GMO corn variety — all of it is cell biology doing exactly what cell biology does. Understand the cell and you can predict what the field will do.

The ladder of life: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. A leaf is an organ. So is your heart. Both are teams of tissues, which are teams of cells.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Cell — the basic unit of life. Everything alive is made of one or more.
  • Nucleus — the cell's office: stores the DNA.
  • Mitochondria — the power plants: release energy from food as ATP.
  • Ribosomes — the factories that build proteins.
  • Cell membrane — the gate around every cell. It is selectively permeable: it decides what gets in and out.
  • Plant-only parts — three things a plant cell has that an animal cell doesn't: a rigid cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens), and a big central vacuole full of water that keeps the plant standing stiff. When the vacuole loses water, the plant wilts.
  • Diffusion — molecules spreading from where they're crowded to where they're not. Costs the cell no energy.
  • Osmosis — diffusion of WATER across a membrane. Water always moves toward the saltier side.
  • Plasmolysis — a plant cell in salty water losing water and shrinking away from its own wall. This is what a "fertilizer burn" actually is.
  • Active transport — moving something AGAINST the crowd, uphill. Costs energy. Root cells do this all day to pull in minerals.
  • Phagocytosis — a cell engulfing a large particle whole, like a white blood cell swallowing a bacterium.
  • DNA — the instruction book, shaped like a twisted ladder: a double helix. The rungs are base pairs: A pairs with T, C pairs with G — always.
  • Gene — one stretch of DNA that codes for one protein. Genes sit along chromosomes.
  • Transcription — copying a gene into messenger RNA (mRNA) inside the nucleus. RNA uses U (uracil) where DNA used T.
  • Translation — a ribosome reading the mRNA three bases at a time. Each three-base codon calls for one amino acid, and the chain folds into a protein.
  • Mutation — a change in the DNA letters. Change one base and the protein can change — the raw material of both natural variation and genetic engineering.

🧱 Activity 5.1.1 — Build the Cell

Two cells, side by side: one from a corn leaf, one from a steer's muscle.

  1. Put the ladder of life in order from simplest to most complex: organ · cell · organism · organ system · tissue.
  2. Match the organelle to its job: nucleus · mitochondria · ribosome · cell membrane → builds proteins · stores the DNA · decides what enters and leaves · releases energy as ATP.
  3. List the THREE parts the corn cell has that the muscle cell doesn't — and for each, say what the corn plant would lose without it.
  4. A drought week hits. Explain a wilted corn plant using the words central vacuole and water — what physically changed inside millions of cells at once?
  5. Both cells have mitochondria. Why does a plant — which makes its own food in chloroplasts — still need power plants too?
Think it through: the cell membrane is selectively permeable — a gate, not a wall. Why would a cell that let EVERYTHING in and out be dead within minutes?

🧂 Activity 5.1.2 — Water Follows Salt (run this one for real)

Osmosis is the single most farm-relevant idea in this lesson, and you can watch it happen tonight with a potato:

  • Cut two potato sticks about the size of french fries (same size as each other — that's your controlled variable from Unit 3). Drop one in a cup of plain water, one in a cup of water with two big spoons of salt stirred in. Wait a few hours or overnight.
  • Feel them: the plain-water stick stays firm or gets firmer; the salt-water stick goes limp and bendy. Water left its cells, moving toward the saltier side.
  1. Report what each stick felt like, and explain both results using osmosis.
  2. The limp stick's cells just did something with a name from the vocabulary list. Which word — and what shrank away from what?
  3. A grower dumps double-strength soluble fertilizer around a tomato plant "to help it grow faster." The plant wilts by evening. Explain the burn — where did the water in the root cells go?
  4. Lesson 4.2/4.3 callback: salty irrigation water slowly poisons an arid field. Connect that to what you just watched in the cup — what is the salt doing to the ROOT cells all season long?
  5. Roots also pull in minerals that are MORE concentrated inside the root than in the soil — uphill. Which transport process is that, and what does it cost the cell?
One rule, three scales: "water follows salt" explains a limp french fry, a fertilizer burn, and a salted-out field. Write the rule in your own words in one sentence.

🧬 Activity 5.1.3 — Crack the Code

Every protein in every crop and animal gets built the same two-step way: DNA → mRNA → protein.

  1. One strand of DNA reads A T G C C A. Write the complementary DNA strand. (Remember the pairing rule — it never changes.)
  2. Now transcribe the ORIGINAL strand into mRNA. What letter shows up that DNA never uses?
  3. Your mRNA is 12 bases long. How many codons is that, and how many amino acids will the ribosome chain together?
  4. Put the whole assembly line in order and name WHERE each step happens: translation at the ______ · transcription in the ______.
  5. Why does the A-with-T, C-with-G rule mean DNA can copy itself accurately every time a cell divides? (What does one strand "know" about the other?)
Lesson 2.1 callback: back in Unit 2 you compared selective breeding and GMO. Now you can say what a GMO actually IS at the code level: engineers change or insert a ______, which changes the ______ the ribosomes build. Fill in both blanks — then say in one sentence why a single changed base can matter to a whole plant.

🍓 Project 5.1.4 — Pull Real DNA Out of a Strawberry

DNA isn't an idea in a diagram — it's a physical thing you can hold. Extract some in your kitchen:

  • Mash one strawberry (or half a banana) in a zip-top bag until smooth.
  • Mix your extraction liquid: half a cup of water + a teaspoon of salt + a big squirt of dish soap. Add it to the bag, squish gently for a minute (soap breaks open cell membranes — you know from this lesson why that releases what's in the nucleus).
  • Strain the mush through a coffee filter or paper towel into a clear cup.
  • Pour cold rubbing alcohol slowly down the side of the cup so it floats as a layer on top. Wait one minute: white, stringy strands rise into the alcohol layer. That's DNA — thousands of strawberry chromosomes' worth, tangled together.
  • Lift the strands with a toothpick. Photograph or sketch what you got.
  1. Report each step and what you observed — including the step where the soap earned its keep.
  2. Why did the recipe need soap? Answer with the organelle-and-membrane words from this lesson.
  3. The strands are visible because millions of copies clumped. Where in the cell was each copy stored before you mashed it?
Honesty rule (same as Units 3 and 4): report what you actually saw. "Cloudy cup, no visible strands, alcohol probably wasn't cold enough" is a real result — and diagnosing WHY it failed is better science than pretending it worked.
