Unit 6 · Agriscience III

Lesson 6.2 — Growing: From Seed to Harvest

Work through this on your own. Do each step, answer the questions, and turn in your work.

🌾 What this lesson is about

In Lesson 3.2 you sprouted kitchen beans and ran a real investigation. This lesson is the story of what that seed was DOING — and everything that happens after it: what a growing plant needs, how it knows which way is up, when it decides to flower, and why the clock keeps running even after harvest.

Big idea: a grower doesn't make plants grow. Plants grow themselves — the grower's whole job is supplying the six things they need, on time, and then getting the crop out of the field before quality slips away.

The six needs: light · water · temperature · air · nutrients · room to grow. Take away ANY one and the crop stalls, no matter how perfect the other five are.

📖 The words you'll use

  • Germination — a seed waking up: it takes in water, and with oxygen and the right temperature, growth starts.
  • Radicle — the embryonic root. It ALWAYS emerges first, before the shoot.
  • Germination test — sprout a counted sample on damp paper and figure the percentage that comes up. How seed is actually graded and sold.
  • Photoperiod — day length. It's the calendar plants read: short-day plants (poinsettias) flower as nights get long; long-day plants (spinach) flower as days stretch out.
  • Light intensity / quality / duration — how bright, which colors, and how long. All three matter, in different ways.
  • Tropism — a growth response toward or away from a stimulus.
  • Phototropism — bending toward light, driven by the hormone auxin building up on the shaded side.
  • Gravitropism — roots grow down, shoots grow up — even in the dark.
  • Thigmotropism — a tendril coiling around whatever it touches.
  • Vegetative growth — the leaves-stems-roots stage, before flowering.
  • Field heat — the warmth a crop carries out of the field at harvest. Pull it out fast, then hold the cold chain, or quality slides.

☑️ Activity 6.2.1 — The Six Needs (and the calendar plants read)

  1. For each problem, name the missing NEED: (a) seedlings in a dark garage grow pale and stretched · (b) a waterlogged pot with no drainage kills the roots — they drowned, but not from too much water itself · (c) a July lettuce planting in Camp Verde bolts and stalls · (d) twenty corn seeds in one small pot come up, then all stay tiny.
  2. Light works three ways — intensity, quality, duration. Match each grower move to the one it controls: shade cloth over summer tomatoes · grow-lights with red and blue LEDs · blackout curtains in a poinsettia greenhouse each afternoon.
  3. Poinsettias must be red by December — so greenhouses give them long artificial nights starting in fall. Spinach bolts to seed in late spring. Using "short-day plant" and "long-day plant," explain both behaviors.
  4. Temperature sets the speed of every internal reaction. Sketch or describe the shape of a growth-vs-temperature curve: what happens below the optimum, at it, and past it? Where do Arizona summers land for a cool-season crop? (This is WHY Yuma grows lettuce in winter — Lesson 1.1, full circle.)
Think it through: a friend says "more of everything = more growth, so I fertilize double and water double." Using the six needs — and what drowning roots tell you about air — explain what's wrong with that plan.

🧪 Activity 6.2.2 — Run a Real Germination Test

This is the actual test seed companies run — and you already have the skills from Lesson 3.2. Takes about 5 days; start it now:

  • Count out exactly 10 seeds (dry beans, lentils, or popcorn from the kitchen all work).
  • Fold them into a damp paper towel, slide it into an open plastic bag (air gets in — remember why), and keep it somewhere warm.
  • Check daily. Keep the towel damp, never soaked.
  1. Record a daily count: how many sprouted by day 2, 3, 4, 5?
  2. What structure came out of every seed FIRST? (Look closely — this is the radicle doing its job.)
  3. Calculate your germination percentage: sprouted ÷ 10 × 100. A seed lot at 90% or better is commercial grade. Did yours pass? (Old kitchen seed often doesn't — if yours came out low, say what might explain it: age, storage heat, the popcorn was roasted?)
  4. Your test used 10 seeds; a seed lab uses 400. Using your sample-size answer from Lesson 3.2's investigation, whose percentage deserves more trust, and why?
  5. A farmer's seed tests at 80% germination and they want 30,000 living plants per acre. About how many seeds per acre must they plant? Show the math.
The three keys: your towel supplied water, the open bag supplied oxygen, the warm spot supplied temperature. Notice what's NOT on the list — light. Explain how a seed can germinate in total darkness underground (where's the energy coming from? Hint: what is a seed mostly made of?).

🧭 Activity 6.2.3 — Tropism Watch (your seedlings know which way is up)

Use a couple of your sprouted seeds from the germination test — they're about to prove they can navigate:

  • Gravity test: take one sprouted seed whose root is clearly pointing one direction. Lay it sideways (root pointing horizontally) on the damp towel and leave it overnight.
  • Light test (2–3 days): pot one sprout in a cup of soil (or keep it on damp towel in a jar) and set it by a window that gets light from ONE side only. Don't rotate it.
  1. Gravity test result: what did the root do within a day of being laid sideways? Which tropism is that — and why is it the reason a farmer never has to worry about which way up a corn planter drops the seed?
  2. Light test result: which way did the stem lean? Explain the mechanism from this lesson's vocabulary: WHERE does auxin build up, and which side of the stem grows faster because of it?
  3. Roots grow DOWN (toward gravity) while shoots grow UP (away from it) — same stimulus, opposite responses, same plant. Why does each direction make survival sense?
  4. Name the third tropism from the vocabulary and one real crop that depends on it. (Think about what beans and grapes climb.)
  5. Change light, water, or heat and the plant answers — stretching, wilting, leaf drop, stalled growth. Pick one of those four responses and explain what the plant is "trying" to accomplish by it.
Design like a scientist (Unit 3 callback): your light test has a flaw — maybe the stem leaned toward the window for some other reason (heat? draft?). Describe the control you'd add to make it a fair test.

🚜 Activity 6.2.4 — Harvest Is a Race Against the Clock

The plant's life cycle runs seed → vegetative growth → flowering → pollination and fertilization → fruit and seed. The grower's job ends with one more step: getting quality OUT of the field intact.

  1. Put these in order for one tomato plant's season, from first to last: fruit set · germination · flowering · vegetative growth · pollination.
  2. Alfalfa is cut near early bloom — not before, not well after. A rancher waits two extra weeks "to get more tonnage." They got more tons; what did they lose? (Quality vs quantity — the trade-off every hay grower manages.)
  3. Baled hay above about 18–20% moisture can mold — and heat until it combusts. Lesson 6.1 told you where that heat comes from. A grower's moisture meter reads 24% at dawn. Should they bale now or wait for afternoon? Explain using both the moisture number AND what desert afternoon air does to drying hay.
  4. Yuma lettuce is cut before sunrise, vacuum-cooled within hours, and trucked refrigerated the whole way. Name the two cold-chain ideas from the vocabulary this routine is protecting — and what happens to a load that sits on hot asphalt for an afternoon instead.
Follow one head of lettuce: from a January Yuma field to a salad in Michigan — list every point where field heat or a broken cold chain could ruin it. Count them. That's how many chances there are to lose the whole paycheck after the crop was already grown perfectly.

📋 Project 6.2.5 — One Crop, Whole Season: the Grow Plan

Be the grower. Pick ONE real crop you could actually grow in the Verde Valley (vegetable garden scale is fine — tomato, corn, beans, spinach, melon, your call):

  • The six needs, itemized: for YOUR crop, how will you supply each of the six? Be specific (which month you plant and why — temperature; how you water — Unit 4 methods; spacing — room).
  • Seed plan: you'll run a germination test first. Say what percentage you'd accept, and how you'd adjust planting if the seed tests low (use the Activity 6.2.2 math).
  • The calendar: map the life cycle onto real months — planting, vegetative stage, flowering, harvest window. Flag the one month where Arizona heat is your crop's biggest enemy and what you'll do about it.
  • Harvest plan: what's your "cut at early bloom" equivalent — the sign your crop is at peak quality? And what do you do in the first hour after picking to protect it?
Honesty rule (same as every unit): if you're not sure of a date or a number (frost dates, spacing, days-to-maturity), look it up and cite where you found it — or state your best guess AS a guess. A grow plan built on named assumptions can be fixed; one built on quiet guesses fails in the field.
